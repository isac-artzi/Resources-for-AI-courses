1. The agent–environment interface

Reinforcement learning is not a family of algorithms first — it is a problem statement, and the algorithms follow from it. The problem is described by a Markov decision process, the five-tuple

\[ \mathcal{M} = \langle \mathcal{S},\, \mathcal{A},\, P,\, R,\, \gamma \rangle, \]

where \(\mathcal{S}\) is a set of states, \(\mathcal{A}\) a set of actions, \(P(s' \mid s,a)\) a transition kernel, \(R(s,a,s')\) a reward function, and \(\gamma \in [0,1]\) a discount factor. At each step \(t\) the agent observes \(S_t\), takes \(A_t\), and the environment responds with \(R_{t+1}\) and \(S_{t+1}\). The interaction produces a trajectory

\[ \tau = (S_0, A_0, R_1, S_1, A_1, R_2, S_2, \dots). \]

A policy \(\pi(a \mid s)\) is a distribution over actions given a state; it is the only thing the agent controls, and it is the only thing that ever gets deployed.

The load-bearing assumption is in the letter \(P\). The transition kernel conditions on \(s\) and \(a\) alone — not on how the agent arrived at \(s\). That is the Markov property:

\[ \Pr(S_{t+1} = s' \mid S_t, A_t, S_{t-1}, A_{t-1}, \dots) = \Pr(S_{t+1} = s' \mid S_t, A_t). \]
Why the Markov property is a design decision, not a fact about the world. Almost no real environment is Markov in its raw observations. A single camera frame does not tell you velocity; a single blood-pressure reading does not tell you trend. The property is something you engineer into the state representation — by stacking frames, adding derivatives, or carrying a recurrent summary. When people say "reinforcement learning did not work on my problem," the cause is very often that the state they fed the agent was not Markov and no algorithm could have recovered the missing information. Deciding what goes into \(s\) is the first modelling choice you make, and it is not a small one.

The taxonomy, and why the boundaries are where they are

Two cuts organise the whole field, and both are cuts through the list above.

  • Model-free vs. model-based. Does the agent have (or learn) \(P\) and \(R\)? A model-based method can plan — simulate consequences without acting. A model-free method can only learn from experience it actually collected. Topic 2 puts one of each behind the same API precisely so the difference stops being rhetorical.
  • Value-based vs. policy-based. Does the agent represent a value function and read the policy off it (\(\pi(s) = \arg\max_a Q(s,a)\)), or parameterise \(\pi_\theta\) and optimise it directly? Value-based methods dominate discrete action spaces; policy-based methods handle continuous ones and stochastic optima that no \(\arg\max\) can express. Topics 3 and 4 are the policy-based branch; actor-critic is the admission that you usually want both.

A third distinction cuts across those: on-policy methods learn about the policy they are currently following; off-policy methods learn about one policy from data generated by another. Q-learning, which you will train in §5, is off-policy — that is why it can learn the greedy policy while behaving \(\varepsilon\)-greedily, and it is the same property that makes the importance-sampling corrections in Topic 3 necessary.

Where this sits relative to supervised learning. Supervised learning receives \((x, y)\) pairs where \(y\) is the correct answer. Reinforcement learning receives \((s, a, r)\) where \(r\) evaluates the action taken and says nothing about the action that should have been taken. This is the difference between instructive and evaluative feedback, and three consequences follow immediately: the agent must explore to discover alternatives, credit for a reward must be assigned backwards across time, and the data distribution is not fixed — it shifts as the policy changes. Every technique in this course exists to manage one of those three.

2. Return, and why we discount

The agent does not maximise reward. It maximises return — the cumulative discounted reward from a point in time onward:

\[ G_t \;=\; R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \gamma^2 R_{t+3} + \cdots \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^{k} R_{t+k+1}. \]

Two facts about \(\gamma\) matter more than the definition. First, if rewards are bounded by \(R_{\max}\) and \(\gamma < 1\), the series converges: \(|G_t| \le R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)\). Without discounting, a non-terminating task has infinite return for every policy, and every policy is therefore equally good — the objective is degenerate. Second, \(\gamma\) sets a soft horizon. The weight on a reward \(k\) steps away is \(\gamma^k\), and the sum of the weights is \(1/(1-\gamma)\), so it is standard to read

\[ H_{\text{eff}} \;=\; \frac{1}{1-\gamma} \]

as the effective number of steps the agent is looking ahead. At \(\gamma = 0.9\) that is ten steps; at \(\gamma = 0.99\) it is a hundred. This is not a minor tuning knob — it decides which problem you are solving.

Discounting is doing three jobs at once, and they are often confused. (i) Mathematical: it makes the infinite sum converge and the Bellman operator a contraction, which is what guarantees the algorithms in §3 terminate at all. (ii) Modelling: it expresses a genuine preference for sooner rewards — the standard interpretation is a per-step \(1-\gamma\) probability that the episode simply ends. (iii) Statistical: a smaller \(\gamma\) truncates the credit-assignment chain, which lowers the variance of every sample-based estimate at the cost of bias. Practitioners reach for job (iii) far more often than they admit — a \(\gamma\) chosen "because it trained better" is a variance-reduction decision wearing a modelling costume. Say which one you are doing when you defend your choice in the engineering report.

3. The Bellman equations and value iteration

Define the state-value function of a policy as the expected return from a state, and the action-value function as the expected return from taking a particular action first:

\[ v_\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[ G_t \mid S_t = s \right], \qquad q_\pi(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[ G_t \mid S_t = s, A_t = a \right]. \]

Split the return into its first term and the rest, \(G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1}\), take expectations, and the recursion writes itself. This is the Bellman expectation equation:

\[ v_\pi(s) \;=\; \sum_{a} \pi(a\mid s) \sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)\Big[ R(s,a,s') + \gamma\, v_\pi(s') \Big]. \]

Replacing the average over actions with a maximum gives the Bellman optimality equation, which the optimal value function \(v_*\) uniquely satisfies:

\[ v_*(s) \;=\; \max_{a} \sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)\Big[ R(s,a,s') + \gamma\, v_*(s') \Big]. \]

Value iteration simply turns that equality into an assignment and applies it repeatedly: \(V_{k+1}(s) \leftarrow \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)[R + \gamma V_k(s')]\). The operator is a \(\gamma\)-contraction in the max norm, \(\lVert TV - TV' \rVert_\infty \le \gamma \lVert V - V' \rVert_\infty\), so by the Banach fixed-point theorem the iterates converge to the unique fixed point \(v_*\) geometrically, from any initialisation.

Why the slippery variant is genuinely a different problem, not a harder one. That is not the deterministic policy executed more carefully — it is a different policy, and it is why "just add noise at test time" is not a substitute for training under the transition kernel you will actually face. Your product's README is asked for one sentence on this; the honest sentence is that stochasticity changes the argmax, not merely the value.

4. Exploration versus exploitation, quantified

Value iteration had \(P\) handed to it. An agent that must learn from experience faces a problem that has no analogue in supervised learning: the only way to find out whether an untried action is good is to try it, and every step spent trying is a step not spent earning. The cleanest setting for measuring this is the \(k\)-armed bandit — a single state, \(k\) actions, each paying from an unknown distribution with mean \(\mu_a\). Performance is measured by cumulative regret against the best arm:

\[ \mathcal{R}_T \;=\; T\mu_{*} - \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \mu_{A_t} \right], \qquad \mu_* = \max_a \mu_a. \]

An \(\varepsilon\)-greedy agent takes the empirical best arm with probability \(1-\varepsilon\) and a uniformly random arm otherwise. Its asymptotic per-step regret does not vanish: it keeps paying \(\varepsilon \cdot \frac{k-1}{k} \cdot \overline{\Delta}\) forever, where \(\overline{\Delta}\) is the mean suboptimality gap. So \(\mathcal{R}_T\) grows linearly. Upper-confidence-bound selection instead picks

\[ A_t \;=\; \arg\max_a \left[ \hat{\mu}_a + c\sqrt{\tfrac{\ln t}{N_a}} \right], \]

an optimism-under-uncertainty rule whose bonus shrinks as an arm is sampled, giving \(\mathcal{R}_T = O(\log T)\) — exploration that pays for itself and then stops.

Why an \(\varepsilon\) that never decays is a bug you can compute the cost of. Suppose you run 20,000 episodes at a fixed \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) on four actions. In expectation \(7.5\%\) of all actions are exploratory and suboptimal, forever — including after the value estimates have long since converged. That is a permanent tax on the behaviour policy. It does not corrupt what Q-learning learns, because Q-learning is off-policy and learns the greedy policy regardless (§5) — but it does corrupt every learning curve you plot from behaviour returns, which is why the evaluation in §6 has to run a separate greedy pass with exploration switched off. Students who report training returns as if they were performance are reporting the tax, not the agent.

5. Q-learning: train it yourself

Q-learning estimates \(q_*\) directly from sampled transitions, with no model of \(P\). After each transition \((s,a,r,s')\) it applies

\[ Q(s,a) \;\leftarrow\; Q(s,a) + \alpha\Big[\underbrace{r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a')}_{\text{TD target}} - Q(s,a)\Big]. \]

The bracketed quantity is the temporal-difference error. Two things deserve attention. The target uses \(\max_{a'}\), the value of the greedy policy, while the action \(a\) that generated the transition came from the \(\varepsilon\)-greedy behaviour policy — that mismatch is what makes Q-learning off-policy. And the update is a sampled, incremental version of exactly the Bellman optimality backup from §3: value iteration with \(P\) replaced by one sample and the assignment replaced by a step of size \(\alpha\) toward it.

Convergence to \(q_*\) with probability 1 requires the Robbins–Monro conditions on the step sizes, \(\sum_t \alpha_t = \infty\) and \(\sum_t \alpha_t^2 < \infty\), plus every state–action pair being visited infinitely often — which is the formal reason exploration cannot be switched off entirely during training.

Why the two curves in that chart are not measuring the same thing. The blue curve is what the behaviour policy earned while exploring; the green points are what the greedy policy earns when you stop. Early in training the blue curve is higher-variance and lower — exploration costs returns. Late in training, with a fixed \(\varepsilon\), the blue curve plateaus below the green points and stays there permanently: the gap is the exploration tax computed in §4. Your product logs the per-episode training returns to the episodes table and the greedy results to evaluations — two tables, because they answer two questions. Reporting the first as if it were the second is the single most common error in submitted work.

6. Measuring an agent honestly

Suppose two configurations are trained and you must recommend one. The temptation is to compare final mean return. But reinforcement learning results are the noisiest in applied machine learning: the same code, the same hyperparameters, and a different random seed can produce qualitatively different curves. A comparison from a single seed is not weak evidence — it is, in the strict sense, no evidence about the configuration at all, because it has not separated the configuration's effect from the seed's.

Report an interval, not a point. With \(n\) seeds and per-seed mean returns \(x_1,\dots,x_n\), the standard error of the mean is \(s/\sqrt{n}\) and an approximate 95% interval is

\[ \bar{x} \;\pm\; t_{0.975,\,n-1}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}. \]

Note the \(\sqrt{n}\): halving the width of the interval costs four times the seeds. Note also that this interval describes the mean across seeds, which is the quantity a claim about the configuration must be about.

And choose the metric deliberately, because different metrics rank configurations differently. Mean final return hides how long you waited for it. Episodes-to-threshold hides what happens after the threshold, including collapse. Success rate hides the magnitude of both successes and failures. Each is defensible; none is neutral.

What the run-history table buys you. Every quantity above is a GROUP BY over rows you already wrote. Mean return with a confidence band is an aggregate over episodes grouped by seed; episodes-to-threshold is the first episode index per seed whose moving average crosses a value; the failed-hyperparameter curve your report must include is a run you did not delete. This is the argument for the database tier that is stronger in reinforcement learning than anywhere else in the programme: the evidence for a policy is not the policy, it is the learning history, and a policy artifact with no history behind it cannot be defended to anyone — including you, three weeks later.

7. In practice (Lake Pilot)

In the build. Every quantity in this tutorial becomes a column, an endpoint, or a tab. The \(\varepsilon\) in force at each episode is logged alongside the return, so §4's exploration tax is recomputable from your data. The greedy evaluation of §6 runs server-side behind POST /rollout and lands in evaluations. The \(Q\)-table of §5 is exported to a NumPy .npz archive, registered in policies with its byte size and SHA-256, and loaded by the service tier — which never imports PyTorch, because it does not need to. A lookup table is a lookup table.

# train/export.py — the artifact you deploy is not the training graph
import numpy as np, hashlib, pathlib

def export_q(Q, path="policies/q_frozenlake_v1.npz"):
    p = pathlib.Path(path); p.parent.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
    np.savez_compressed(p, Q=Q.astype(np.float32))       # float32 halves the artifact
    digest = hashlib.sha256(p.read_bytes()).hexdigest()
    return {"path": str(p), "bytes": p.stat().st_size, "sha256": digest}

# api/policy.py — the entire serving-side forward pass
def act(Q, state: int) -> int:
    return int(Q[state].argmax())                          # no torch, no framework, 1 line
The guard that keeps this honest. tests/test_no_torch.py imports your FastAPI app and asserts "torch" not in sys.modules. It is not busywork: import torch alone costs roughly 490 MB of resident memory, against a 690 MB guarantee on the deployed tier. The measured footprint of the full stack in this course, before any policy loads, is 82 MB. The rule is what keeps that number true through Topic 6.
