Topic 4 — Actor-Critic Methods: From Advantage Actor-Critic to Soft Actor-Critic

One family, three algorithms, and a single organising idea: every method here is a policy gradient whose return has been replaced by an estimate, and whose step has been constrained by a regulariser. A2C replaces the return with an advantage and buys variance reduction. PPO adds a trust region and buys a step you can take without measuring it. SAC adds an entropy term and buys a different optimum. The last of those three is the one Topic 6 is built on, and this tutorial is where it is set up.

1. The critic as a bias–variance dial

Topic 3 ended with the policy gradient theorem in its general form: for any function \(\Psi_t\) of the trajectory,

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\left[ \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, \Psi_t \right], \]

and the choice of \(\Psi_t\) decides everything about how the estimator behaves. REINFORCE takes \(\Psi_t = G_t\), the full sampled return. That choice is unbiased and it is the worst choice available on variance, because \(G_t\) accumulates the randomness of every reward and every transition from \(t\) to the end of the episode. An actor-critic method is precisely the decision to replace that sampled return with an estimate produced by a learned value function — a critic — and then to face the consequences.

The family of estimates is indexed by one integer. The \(n\)-step return takes \(n\) real rewards and then bootstraps:

\[ G^{(n)}_t \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \gamma^{k} R_{t+k+1} \;+\; \gamma^{n} V_\phi(S_{t+n}). \]

At \(n=1\) this is the one-step temporal-difference target of Topic 1, and \(V_\phi\) supplies everything beyond the first reward. At \(n=\infty\) — meaning "run to termination" — the bootstrap term vanishes and the estimate is the Monte Carlo return. Every intermediate \(n\) is a mixture, and the two error sources move in opposite directions as \(n\) grows.

Write the critic's error at a state as \(\delta_\phi(s) = V_\phi(s) - v_\pi(s)\). Taking expectations over the trajectory, and using \(\mathbb{E}[\sum_{k \[ \operatorname{Bias}\!\left[G^{(n)}_t\right] \;=\; \gamma^{n}\, \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \delta_\phi(S_{t+n}) \right]. \]

The critic's error enters attenuated by \(\gamma^n\), so the bias decays geometrically in \(n\) and is exactly zero at \(n = \infty\). The variance runs the other way: each additional sampled reward adds its own noise, and for independent reward noise of variance \(\sigma^2\),

\[ \operatorname{Var}\!\left[G^{(n)}_t\right] \;=\; \sigma^2 \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \gamma^{2k} \;=\; \sigma^2\,\frac{1-\gamma^{2n}}{1-\gamma^{2}}, \]

which increases monotonically in \(n\) and saturates at the Monte Carlo value \(\sigma^2/(1-\gamma^2)\). One number decays, the other grows. There is no setting of \(n\) that is good on both, and which end of the dial you want depends on a quantity you do not know in advance: how wrong your critic currently is.

Why \(n\) is not the knob you actually ship, and what is. The demo makes \(n\) look like a hyperparameter to tune, and it is — but an integer \(n\) commits the estimator to a single point on the dial and throws away every other horizon you already paid to sample. Generalised advantage estimation instead takes an exponentially weighted average over all \(n\) simultaneously, \(\hat{A}^{\text{GAE}(\lambda)}_t = \sum_{l\ge 0}(\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}\) with \(\delta_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma V_\phi(S_{t+1}) - V_\phi(S_t)\), which reduces to \(n=1\) at \(\lambda=0\) and to Monte Carlo at \(\lambda=1\). The reason to prefer it is not that it is more accurate at its best setting — it is that the weighted average is computed in one backward pass over the batch you already have, so the dial becomes continuous at no extra sample cost. This is the estimator inside your PPO implementation; \(\lambda = 0.95\) is the default you should be able to justify by pointing at the curves above rather than at a paper.

2. A2C: the advantage, and what it costs

Advantage actor-critic makes one specific choice of \(\Psi_t\): the advantage function

\[ A^\pi(s,a) \;=\; q^\pi(s,a) - v^\pi(s), \]

which asks not "how good is this action" but "how much better than average is this action, in this state". Two claims must be established before the choice is defensible, and they are separate claims: that subtracting \(v^\pi(s)\) does not bias the gradient, and that it reduces its variance.

Unbiasedness: any state-dependent baseline is free

Let \(b(s)\) be any function of the state alone. Then, at a fixed \(s\),

\[ \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\theta}\!\left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\, b(s) \right] \;=\; b(s) \sum_a \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\, \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)} \;=\; b(s)\, \nabla_\theta \sum_a \pi_\theta(a\mid s) \;=\; b(s)\, \nabla_\theta 1 \;=\; 0. \]

The subtracted term has expectation exactly zero, so \(\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi\,(q^\pi - b)] = \mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi\, q^\pi]\) for every \(b\). Note where the argument breaks: \(b\) must not depend on \(a\), because then it could not be pulled outside the sum and \(\nabla_\theta\sum_a \pi_\theta = 0\) would no longer be the identity being used. This is the entire reason baselines are functions of the state.

Variance reduction (DQ 1a)

Write \(X = \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\) for the score at a fixed state and \(Q = q^\pi(s,a)\). Because \(\mathbb{E}[Xb] = 0\), the mean of the estimator is the same for every \(b\), so comparing variances is comparing second moments:

\[ \operatorname{Var}\!\left[X(Q-b)\right] \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\lVert X\rVert^2 (Q-b)^2\right] \;-\; \big\lVert\mathbb{E}[XQ]\big\rVert^2, \]

and only the first term depends on \(b\). Differentiating it with respect to \(b\) and setting the derivative to zero,

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial b}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\lVert X\rVert^2 (Q-b)^2\right] = -2\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\lVert X\rVert^2 (Q-b)\right] = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b^\star = \frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\lVert X\rVert^2 Q\right]}{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\lVert X\rVert^2\right]}. \]

The optimal baseline is a score-weighted average of \(q^\pi\). If \(\lVert X\rVert^2\) and \(Q\) are approximately uncorrelated across actions — the standard working assumption — then \(b^\star \approx \mathbb{E}[Q] = v^\pi(s)\), which is exactly the advantage. Substituting \(b = v^\pi(s)\) and comparing against \(b=0\) gives the reduction explicitly:

\[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\lVert X\rVert^2 Q^2\right] - \mathbb{E}\!\left[\lVert X\rVert^2 (Q-v)^2\right] \;=\; v\left(2\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\lVert X\rVert^2 Q\right] - v\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\lVert X\rVert^2\right]\right) \;\approx\; v^2\, \mathbb{E}\!\left[\lVert X\rVert^2\right] \;\ge\; 0. \]

The saving is proportional to \(v^\pi(s)^2\). That is the practical statement: the advantage helps most exactly where the raw \(q\)-values are large and nearly equal — a state worth \(+200\) in which one action is worth \(+201\) and another \(+199\). Without the baseline every action receives a large positive gradient and the estimator must resolve a \(1\%\) difference through sampling noise of the same magnitude as the values themselves; with it, the two actions receive \(+1\) and \(-1\) and the signal is the whole quantity.

Why the reduction is not automatic, and the one case where the baseline hurts. The inequality above relies on the approximation \(\mathbb{E}[\lVert X\rVert^2 Q] \approx \mathbb{E}[\lVert X\rVert^2]\,v\). Drop it and the exact condition for \(b=v\) to beat \(b=0\) is \(2\,\mathbb{E}[\lVert X\rVert^2 Q] > v\,\mathbb{E}[\lVert X\rVert^2]\), which can fail when the score magnitude is strongly anti-correlated with the action value — when the actions the policy is least certain about are precisely the valuable ones. This is not a pathology you will hit on CartPole, but it is the reason the claim is "the advantage reduces variance under a stated assumption" and not "subtracting anything helps". The second-order cost is more likely to bite you: \(v^\pi\) is not known, so \(A\) is estimated as \(G^{(n)}_t - V_\phi(S_t)\), and the critic's own error is now inside your policy gradient with the bias of §1. A2C trades a large unbiased variance for a smaller biased one, and the trade is only good while the critic is learning faster than the actor is moving.

The performance difference lemma (DQ 2a)

The advantage is not merely a variance-reduction device. It is the exact currency in which policy improvement is measured. Let \(J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{s_0}[v^\pi(s_0)]\). For any two policies \(\pi\) and \(\pi'\),

\[ J(\pi') - J(\pi) \;=\; \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\; \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi'}}\, \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi'(\cdot\mid s)}\!\left[ A^{\pi}(s,a) \right], \]

where \(d^{\pi'}\) is the normalised discounted state-visitation distribution of \(\pi'\). The derivation is a telescoping sum and takes four lines. Start from the trajectory distribution of \(\pi'\) and expand the advantage of the old policy along it:

\[ \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi'}\!\left[\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t A^\pi(s_t,a_t)\right] = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi'}\!\left[\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t \Big(R_{t+1} + \gamma v^\pi(s_{t+1}) - v^\pi(s_t)\Big)\right], \]

using \(A^\pi(s,a) = \mathbb{E}[R + \gamma v^\pi(s') \mid s,a] - v^\pi(s)\). Split the bracket into the reward part and the value part. The reward part is \(J(\pi')\) by definition. The value part telescopes:

\[ \sum_{t\ge 0}\left(\gamma^{t+1} v^\pi(s_{t+1}) - \gamma^{t} v^\pi(s_t)\right) \;=\; -\,v^\pi(s_0), \]

whose expectation is \(-J(\pi)\). Hence \(\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi'}[\sum_t \gamma^t A^\pi(s_t,a_t)] = J(\pi') - J(\pi)\), and rewriting the discounted sum over time as \(\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\) times an expectation over the normalised visitation distribution \(d^{\pi'}\) gives the lemma.

Read what it says. To improve on \(\pi\) you need actions with positive \(A^\pi\) — measured under the state distribution of the new policy, which you cannot sample until you have committed to it. Every practical algorithm makes the same substitution, replacing \(d^{\pi'}\) by \(d^{\pi}\) to obtain the surrogate objective

\[ L_\pi(\pi') \;=\; \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\,\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi}, a\sim \pi}\!\left[ \frac{\pi'(a\mid s)}{\pi(a\mid s)}\, A^{\pi}(s,a) \right], \]

which agrees with the true improvement to first order at \(\pi'=\pi\) and whose error is bounded by a term growing in the divergence between the two policies. Small policy changes are safe because the substitution is accurate for small changes and for no others. That sentence is the entire motivation for §3.

3. Trust regions: TRPO's constraint, PPO's clip

Trust region policy optimisation takes the surrogate at face value and constrains the region on which it is trusted:

\[ \max_{\theta}\;\; \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi_{\text{old}}},\,a\sim\pi_{\text{old}}}\!\left[ \rho_\theta(s,a)\, A^{\pi_{\text{old}}}(s,a) \right] \quad\text{subject to}\quad \bar{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\text{old}} \,\|\, \pi_\theta\right) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot\mid s)\,\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)\right) \right] \;\le\; \delta, \]

with \(\rho_\theta(s,a) = \pi_\theta(a\mid s)/\pi_{\text{old}}(a\mid s)\). Linearising the objective and taking a second-order expansion of the constraint — whose Hessian at \(\theta_{\text{old}}\) is the Fisher information matrix \(F\) — turns this into a problem with a closed-form solution: step along \(F^{-1}g\), the natural gradient, with the largest length the constraint permits,

\[ \theta \;\leftarrow\; \theta_{\text{old}} + \sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{g^{\!\top} F^{-1} g}}\; F^{-1} g. \]

The step is measured in the geometry of the distribution, not of the parameters, which is why it is invariant to how the policy is parameterised. It is also why TRPO is expensive: \(F^{-1}g\) is obtained by conjugate gradient over Fisher-vector products, and the whole procedure is followed by a backtracking line search to confirm the constraint actually holds after the quadratic approximation is discarded.

PPO's clip, derived by cases

Proximal policy optimisation abandons the constraint and modifies the objective so that the incentive to leave the region disappears:

\[ L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\, \min\Big( \rho_\theta A,\; \operatorname{clip}(\rho_\theta,\, 1-\epsilon,\, 1+\epsilon)\, A \Big) \right]. \]

The behaviour is not symmetric in the sign of \(A\), and the asymmetry is the design, not an artefact. Take the two cases separately.

Case \(A > 0\). Multiplying by a positive number preserves order, so \(\min(\rho A, \operatorname{clip}(\rho)A) = A\cdot\min(\rho, \operatorname{clip}(\rho)) = A\cdot\min(\rho,\,1+\epsilon)\). The objective rises linearly in \(\rho\) up to \(1+\epsilon\) and is flat thereafter. The gradient with respect to \(\rho\) is \(A\) for \(\rho < 1+\epsilon\) and exactly \(0\) for \(\rho > 1+\epsilon\). The update pushes the probability of a good action up, and stops pushing once the action has become \((1+\epsilon)\) times as likely as it was.

Case \(A < 0\). Multiplying by a negative number reverses order, so \(\min(\rho A, \operatorname{clip}(\rho)A) = A\cdot\max(\rho, \operatorname{clip}(\rho)) = A\cdot\max(\rho,\,1-\epsilon)\). Now the flat region is below \(1-\epsilon\): the gradient is \(A\) for \(\rho > 1-\epsilon\) and \(0\) for \(\rho < 1-\epsilon\). The update pushes the probability of a bad action down, and stops once it has fallen to \((1-\epsilon)\) of its old value.

Two consequences follow that a symmetric penalty would not produce. First, the flat region is always on the side the update wants to move toward, so clipping removes the incentive to go further and never removes the incentive to come back: a ratio that has already drifted too far still receives full gradient in the corrective direction. Second, because \(\min\) is taken rather than \(\operatorname{clip}\) alone, \(L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}\) is a pessimistic lower bound on the unclipped surrogate — it equals the unclipped value whenever that value is the smaller of the two. For \(A<0\) and a very large \(\rho\), \(L^{\mathrm{CLIP}} = \rho A\) is unbounded below, so a single sample that has become far more likely under the new policy and turned out to be bad still dominates the batch loss. The clip bounds the reward for optimism; it does not bound the penalty for a mistake.

Why PPO replaced TRPO in practice, and what was actually given up. TRPO enforces a constraint; PPO removes an incentive. Those are not the same guarantee, and PPO's is strictly weaker — nothing in \(L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}\) prevents \(\bar{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\) from exceeding any bound you had in mind, because several epochs of minibatch gradient steps on a clipped objective can still walk the policy a long way if many samples sit inside the unclipped band. What PPO buys is that the objective is a scalar you can differentiate with a single line of autograd, with no Fisher-vector products, no conjugate gradient, and no line search, which means it composes with minibatching, recurrent policies, and shared actor–critic trunks. The honest engineering summary is that PPO exchanges a certificate for a heuristic that works, and the way you recover evidence for the heuristic is to log the realised mean KL per update — which is exactly why your product logs it. If that series drifts upward across training, your \(\epsilon\) is not doing the job you assumed it was.

4. Off-policy actor-critic and the weight that explodes

Everything so far is on-policy: the data in the batch was generated by the policy being improved, and it is discarded after one update. That is a severe inefficiency. A transition is a costly object — on a physical system it is the only thing you actually pay for — and using it once is a choice, not a necessity. Off-policy actor-critic methods keep a replay buffer \(\mathcal{D}\) of past transitions generated by older policies, or by a separate behaviour policy \(b\), and reuse it.

The correction is importance sampling. For a single decision step,

\[ \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}\!\left[ f(s,a) \right] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{a\sim b}\!\left[ \frac{\pi(a\mid s)}{b(a\mid s)} f(s,a) \right], \]

which gives the off-policy policy gradient used by OffPAC:

\[ \nabla_\theta J \;\approx\; \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{b},\, a\sim b}\!\left[ \frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{b(a\mid s)}\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\, q^{\pi}(s,a) \right]. \]

Note the approximation sign. The action distribution has been corrected; the state distribution has not — the expectation is still over \(d^b\), not \(d^\pi\). OffPAC drops that correction deliberately, and the resulting update is a valid ascent direction on the excursion objective \(J_b(\pi) = \sum_s d^b(s) v^\pi(s)\) rather than on \(J(\pi)\) itself. Whether that is acceptable is a question about how far \(d^b\) has drifted from \(d^\pi\), and it is not answerable from the update rule alone.

The variance of the weights (DQ 7)

The per-step weight \(\rho_t = \pi(a_t|s_t)/b(a_t|s_t)\) has mean 1 under \(b\). Its second moment is \(\mathbb{E}_b[\rho^2] = 1 + \chi^2(\pi \,\|\, b)\), where \(\chi^2\) is the chi-square divergence — zero only when the policies agree. Correcting a whole trajectory requires the product \(\rho_{0:T-1} = \prod_{t \[ \operatorname{Var}\!\left[\rho_{0:T-1}\right] \;=\; \left(1+\chi^2\right)^{T} - 1. \]

The variance grows exponentially in the horizon. A one-percent-per-step mismatch, \(\chi^2 = 0.01\), gives a standard deviation of about \(0.5\) at \(T=25\) and about \(12\) at \(T=200\): the estimator is dominated by a handful of trajectories with enormous weight and is useless long before it is biased. The standard diagnostic is the effective sample size, \(\mathrm{ESS} = (\sum_i w_i)^2 / \sum_i w_i^2\), which reports how many of your \(M\) samples the weighted average is really using.

Three responses exist and you should be able to name which one each algorithm chose. Truncate the weights — V-trace and Retrace clip \(\rho_t\) at a constant, accepting bias to bound variance. Correct per decision rather than per trajectory, so no product is ever formed. Or eliminate the ratio entirely: this is what SAC does. Its critic is trained by a Bellman backup, which is off-policy by construction and needs no ratio; its actor is updated by drawing fresh actions from the current policy at replayed states, so the action distribution is never mismatched and only the state distribution is stale. The third route is why SAC can use a million-transition replay buffer and PPO cannot.

Why sample efficiency and stability are the same axis viewed twice. The natural framing is a trade: on-policy methods are stable and wasteful, off-policy methods are efficient and fragile. The mechanism behind both halves is one quantity — the divergence between the policy that generated the data and the policy being evaluated. On-policy methods hold that divergence near zero by throwing data away, which is what makes them stable and what makes them expensive. Off-policy methods let it grow and must then either correct it (importance weights, whose variance is the \((1+\chi^2)^T\) above), bound it (a trust region), or avoid needing it (a Bellman backup). Your bake-off compares A2C and PPO at matched environment-step budgets precisely so this shows up as a number rather than an adjective: the same budget buys different amounts of progress, and the difference is attributable to how each method spends its divergence allowance.

5. Maximum-entropy RL and the soft Bellman backup

Every method so far treats exploration as something outside the objective — an \(\varepsilon\), a Gaussian with a hand-set standard deviation, an entropy bonus added to the loss as a regulariser. Maximum-entropy reinforcement learning makes it part of what is being maximised:

\[ J(\pi) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[ \sum_{t\ge 0} \gamma^{t} \Big( r(s_t,a_t) \;+\; \alpha\, \mathcal{H}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid s_t)\big) \Big) \right], \qquad \mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid s)) = -\sum_a \pi(a\mid s)\log\pi(a\mid s). \]

The temperature \(\alpha\) has units of reward per nat and sets the exchange rate between the two. This is a different objective, not a different algorithm for the same objective: its optimum is a stochastic policy, and it remains stochastic at convergence.

Soft value functions

Define the soft state-value function as the entropy-augmented expected return and the soft action-value function as the value of committing to one action first:

\[ V(s) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}\!\left[ Q(s,a) - \alpha \log \pi(a\mid s) \right] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[Q(s,a)] + \alpha\,\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid s)), \qquad Q(s,a) \;=\; r(s,a) + \gamma\, \mathbb{E}_{s'}\!\left[ V(s') \right]. \]

The entropy of the current step is charged to \(V\) and not to \(Q\), because \(Q\) conditions on the action already having been chosen. This bookkeeping detail is where implementations most often go wrong.

The optimal policy is Boltzmann

Fix \(s\) and \(Q(s,\cdot)\) and maximise \(V(s)\) over the distribution \(\pi(\cdot\mid s)\). The Lagrangian with the normalisation constraint is

\[ \mathcal{L} = \sum_a \pi(a)Q(s,a) - \alpha\sum_a \pi(a)\log\pi(a) + \lambda\Big(1-\sum_a\pi(a)\Big), \] \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \pi(a)} = Q(s,a) - \alpha\big(\log \pi(a) + 1\big) - \lambda = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \log\pi(a) = \frac{Q(s,a)-\lambda}{\alpha} - 1. \]

The multiplier is absorbed by normalising, giving the Boltzmann (softmax) policy

\[ \pi^{\star}(a\mid s) \;=\; \frac{\exp\!\big(Q(s,a)/\alpha\big)}{\sum_{b}\exp\!\big(Q(s,b)/\alpha\big)} \;\propto\; \exp\!\big(Q(s,a)/\alpha\big). \]

Substituting \(\pi^\star\) back into \(V\) collapses the expression. With \(Z = \sum_b \exp(Q(s,b)/\alpha)\) and \(\log\pi^\star(a) = Q(s,a)/\alpha - \log Z\),

\[ V(s) = \sum_a \pi^\star(a)Q(s,a) - \alpha\sum_a\pi^\star(a)\left(\frac{Q(s,a)}{\alpha} - \log Z\right) = \alpha\log Z, \]

so the soft Bellman backup for the state-value function is the log-sum-exp of the action values (DQ 4a):

\[ \boxed{\;V(s) \;=\; \alpha \log \sum_{a} \exp\!\left( \frac{Q(s,a)}{\alpha} \right), \qquad Q(s,a) = r(s,a) + \gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'}[V(s')].\;} \]

The log-sum-exp is a soft maximum

The claim that this operator generalises \(\max\) is provable in two lines, not asserted. Let \(m = \max_a Q(s,a)\) and \(|\mathcal{A}|\) be the number of actions. Keeping only the largest term gives a lower bound; replacing every term by the largest gives an upper bound:

\[ m \;=\; \alpha\log \exp(m/\alpha) \;\le\; \alpha\log\sum_a \exp\!\big(Q(s,a)/\alpha\big) \;\le\; \alpha\log\Big(|\mathcal{A}|\exp(m/\alpha)\Big) \;=\; m + \alpha\log|\mathcal{A}|. \]

The gap is at most \(\alpha \log|\mathcal{A}|\), so as \(\alpha\to 0^+\) the operator is squeezed onto \(\max\) and the Boltzmann policy onto the greedy one — the soft Bellman equation becomes the ordinary Bellman optimality equation. In the opposite limit, expanding \(\exp(Q/\alpha)\approx 1 + Q/\alpha\) gives \(\alpha\log\sum_a\exp(Q/\alpha) \to \alpha\log|\mathcal{A}| + \bar{Q} + O(1/\alpha)\), where \(\bar{Q}\) is the unweighted mean: the action values stop mattering and the policy goes uniform. The two limits are the two failure modes an entropy sweep is designed to find.

The soft policy gradient theorem

For a parameterised policy the improvement step is a projection: choose \(\pi_\theta\) to minimise the divergence to the Boltzmann distribution induced by the current critic,

\[ \pi_{\text{new}} = \arg\min_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left( \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s) \;\Big\|\; \frac{\exp(Q(s,\cdot)/\alpha)}{Z(s)} \right) \right], \]

which, dropping the \(\theta\)-independent \(\log Z(s)\), is the objective \(J_\pi(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{D}}\,\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\theta}\!\left[\alpha\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s) - Q(s,a)\right]\). In score-function form the gradient is the ordinary policy gradient with the reward replaced by the entropy-augmented one,

\[ \nabla_\theta J \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\Big( Q(s,a) - \alpha\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s) - b(s) \Big) \right], \]

but SAC uses the lower-variance reparameterised form instead. Writing \(a = f_\theta(\epsilon; s)\) for a deterministic function of a noise sample \(\epsilon\),

\[ \nabla_\theta J_\pi \;=\; \nabla_\theta\, \alpha\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s) \;+\; \Big( \nabla_{a}\,\alpha\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s) \;-\; \nabla_{a} Q(s,a) \Big)\, \nabla_\theta f_\theta(\epsilon;s), \]

where the first term differentiates the explicit \(\theta\)-dependence at fixed \(a\) and the second flows the critic's gradient through the sampled action. The reparameterisation is what makes the estimator usable on continuous actions: it differentiates through the sample rather than weighting it.

Why the values inflate with \(\alpha\) and why that is not a bug to normalise away. Raise \(\alpha\) in the demo and every value rises, roughly by \(\alpha\log|\mathcal{A}|/(1-\gamma)\), while the differences between states shrink. The obvious reading is that the heat map has been corrupted by an additive constant that should be subtracted off. It has not: the agent really is collecting \(\alpha\) units of reward per nat of entropy at every step forever, and that stream is part of the objective it was told to maximise. The consequence is the one your entropy sweep must detect — at large \(\alpha\) the entropy income dwarfs the task reward, the value differences between good and bad states fall below the critic's own approximation error, and the policy has no gradient left to distinguish them. A policy that is "exploring" at \(\alpha = 0.5\) on Pendulum is not being cautious; it is optimising a different problem in which the task reward is a rounding error. That is why the sweep reports mean policy entropy alongside mean return: the two together identify which of the two regimes a run is in, and neither alone can.

6. SAC: temperature, squashing, and two regularisers

Soft actor-critic is the maximum-entropy objective made into an off-policy algorithm: twin \(Q\)-networks with a minimum taken over them to control overestimation, target networks, a replay buffer, a reparameterised squashed Gaussian actor, and an automatically tuned \(\alpha\). Three of those pieces need derivation.

Automatic temperature tuning

Fixing \(\alpha\) by hand is unworkable because it is a reward-scale-dependent quantity: doubling the environment's rewards halves the effective temperature. Instead, state the problem as constrained — maximise return subject to the policy retaining at least \(\bar{\mathcal{H}}\) nats of entropy on average — and let \(\alpha\) be the dual variable. The dual objective, for a fixed policy, is

\[ J(\alpha) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{D},\,a\sim\pi}\!\left[ -\alpha \big( \log\pi(a\mid s) + \bar{\mathcal{H}} \big) \right] \;=\; \alpha\left( \mathcal{H}(\pi) - \bar{\mathcal{H}} \right), \]

whose gradient is \(\partial J/\partial\alpha = \mathcal{H}(\pi) - \bar{\mathcal{H}}\). Descending it gives \(\alpha \leftarrow \alpha - \lambda(\mathcal{H}(\pi) - \bar{\mathcal{H}})\): when the policy is more random than the target, the price of entropy falls; when it has collapsed below the target, the price rises and pushes it back. The standard heuristic target for a continuous action space is \(\bar{\mathcal{H}} = -\dim(\mathcal{A})\), which for Pendulum's single torque dimension is \(-1\) nat. Note that \(\alpha\) must be constrained positive, which is why implementations parameterise \(\log\alpha\) and optimise that.

The tanh-squashed Gaussian and its log-probability correction

Pendulum's action is a bounded torque. A Gaussian policy is unbounded, so SAC samples an unconstrained \(u \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(s), \sigma_\theta(s))\) and squashes it, \(a = \tanh(u)\). Squashing is a change of variables, and a density does not survive one unchanged. For an invertible map the densities relate by the Jacobian determinant,

\[ \pi(a\mid s) \;=\; \mathcal{N}(u \mid s)\,\left| \det\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}a}{\mathrm{d}u}\right) \right|^{-1}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}a_i}{\mathrm{d}u_i} = 1-\tanh^2(u_i), \]

and since the map acts coordinatewise the Jacobian is diagonal and its determinant is a product. Taking logs,

\[ \log\pi(a\mid s) \;=\; \log\mathcal{N}(u\mid s) \;-\; \sum_i \log\!\left(1-\tanh^2(u_i)\right). \]

The correction is not a numerical nicety. Without it the quantity you are calling a log-probability does not integrate to one over \((-1,1)^{\dim\mathcal{A}}\), so the entropy term in the objective and the \(\alpha\) it is tuned against are both measuring the wrong thing — and the error is largest exactly at the saturated actions the policy will drift toward. In code, \(\log(1-\tanh^2 u) = 2\big(\log 2 - u - \mathrm{softplus}(-2u)\big)\) is the numerically stable form; the naive expression underflows for \(|u| \gtrsim 10\).

Two regularisers that are constantly confused

PPO and SAC both restrain the policy, and it is common to hear that they therefore do "the same thing". They do not, and the distinction is the one the rest of this course rests on.

Start from an identity. For a finite action set with uniform reference \(U(a) = 1/|\mathcal{A}|\),

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi \,\|\, U\right) \;=\; \sum_a \pi(a)\log\frac{\pi(a)}{1/|\mathcal{A}|} \;=\; \sum_a \pi(a)\log\pi(a) \;+\; \log|\mathcal{A}| \;=\; -\,\mathcal{H}(\pi) \;+\; \log|\mathcal{A}|. \]

Substituting into the maximum-entropy objective,

\[ r + \alpha\,\mathcal{H}(\pi) \;=\; r \;-\; \alpha\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi\,\|\,U\right) \;+\; \alpha\log|\mathcal{A}|, \]

and the final term is constant in \(\pi\), so it shifts the objective's value without moving its argmax. Maximising reward plus \(\alpha\) times entropy is exactly maximising reward minus \(\alpha\) times a KL divergence against a uniform reference policy. Entropy regularisation is KL regularisation; the reference just happens to be the least informative distribution available.

The trust region is a different object with a different reference. PPO and TRPO constrain \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\text{old}} \,\|\, \pi_\theta)\), where \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) is the previous iterate — a reference that moves after every update. Three consequences separate them cleanly:

  • Fixed point. Entropy changes the optimum: the maximum-entropy optimal policy is stochastic, Boltzmann in \(Q/\alpha\), and stays stochastic forever. A trust region does not change the optimum at all — the optimum of the unregularised problem is still a deterministic policy — it only bounds the path taken toward it. Run PPO long enough and its entropy goes to zero; run SAC long enough at fixed \(\alpha\) and its entropy converges to a positive constant.
  • What is bounded. Entropy bounds the policy's distance from determinism. A trust region bounds the policy's distance from its own previous version. A policy can be far from uniform and inside its trust region, or close to uniform and far outside it; the two quantities are not comparable.
  • Where it lives. The entropy term is inside the objective and inside the critic's backup — it is why \(V(s) = \alpha\log\sum_a\exp(Q/\alpha)\) and not \(\max_a Q\). The trust region is outside the objective, a property of the optimiser, and the value function knows nothing about it.

Now replace the reference. If \(U\) is swapped for a learned reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), the same Lagrangian derivation as §5 returns the same shape of answer with the reference multiplying in:

\[ \max_\pi\; \mathbb{E}[r] - \beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \pi^\star(a\mid s) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(s)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(a\mid s)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{r(s,a)}{\beta}\right), \]

which reduces to the Boltzmann policy of §5 exactly when \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is uniform, because a constant factor is absorbed by \(Z\). This is the objective of reinforcement learning from human feedback, and \(\beta\) plays the role \(\alpha\) plays here. Topic 6 introduces no new mathematics on this point; it changes one distribution in a formula you have already derived.

Why the identity is worth this much attention when the constant \(\log|\mathcal{A}|\) makes it look trivial. The temptation is to treat \(-\mathcal{H}(\pi) + \log|\mathcal{A}| = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|U)\) as an algebraic curiosity, because an additive constant never changes an argmax. What it buys is a change of type. Entropy is a property of one distribution and has no obvious generalisation; a KL divergence is a property of a pair, and the second slot is a free parameter. Once the objective is written as \(r - \alpha D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\), the reference can be a pretrained language model, a safe baseline controller, or last week's deployed policy, and every derivation in §5 survives verbatim: the optimum is still exponential-tilted, the soft value is still a log-partition function, and the temperature is still a dual variable. The finite-action identity is the bridge, and it is the reason the syllabus can claim that Topic 6 introduces no new mathematics. A student who reads the entropy term as "a bonus that encourages exploration" has the right intuition and the wrong object, and will not recognise the same equation when it reappears with \(\beta\) in place of \(\alpha\).

7. In practice (Control Bench)

In the build. Three agents, one contract. A2C trains on CartPole-v1, PPO on Acrobot-v1, SAC on Pendulum-v1 — all classic control, all pure wheels, all comfortably inside the free-tier allocation. One POST /act endpoint serves whichever policy the caller names in the request body, and the three differ only in the shape of what comes out: a categorical sample for the first two, a squashed mean for the third. Every episode goes to episodes; PPO additionally logs the realised mean KL per update, so the trust-region claim of §3 is checkable against data rather than asserted in prose; SAC logs critic loss and mean policy entropy, so the two regimes named in §5 are distinguishable in the table.

The entropy_sweep table is the topic's central experiment, and its shape follows from what the sweep must establish. Three regimes — \(\alpha = 0.5\), \(\alpha = 0.01\), and automatic tuning against \(\bar{\mathcal{H}} = -1\) — crossed with at least three seeds each, reporting convergence speed, final performance, and stability:

Why POST /act must return 422 and not a stack trace

The three policies take observation vectors of dimension 4 (CartPole), 6 (Acrobot), and 3 (Pendulum). A caller who names sac_pendulum and sends a four-element state has made a client error, and the contract must say so. Passing that array into a NumPy matrix multiply raises a ValueError deep in the forward pass, which FastAPI renders as a 500 — a status code that tells the caller the server is broken and that their request should be retried. It will fail identically every time. Validate the shape at the boundary, name both dimensions in the message, and return 422:

# api/policy.py — the whole serving-side forward pass for SAC, plus the guard
import numpy as np
from fastapi import HTTPException

def act_sac(P, s: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    if s.shape != (int(P["obs_dim"]),):                      # boundary check, before any matmul
        raise HTTPException(422, f"policy expects {int(P['obs_dim'])} dims, got {s.shape[0]}")
    h = np.tanh(P["W1"] @ s + P["b1"]); h = np.tanh(P["W2"] @ h + P["b2"])
    mu = P["Wmu"] @ h + P["bmu"]                            # 1. the mean head
    # log_std = np.clip(P["Wls"] @ h + P["bls"], -20, 2)      # 2. only needed for stochastic rollouts
    return np.tanh(mu) * P["act_scale"]                      # 3. the squash, and the torque limit

That is the "three extra lines" the build step refers to: a mean head, a log-standard-deviation head, and a tanh. Deterministic evaluation uses \(\tanh(\mu)\) and never touches the log-standard deviation; only stochastic rollouts sample \(u = \mu + \sigma\epsilon\) first. Your PyTorch/NumPy equivalence test must pin both paths, because they exercise different tensors and a transposed weight matrix will pass one and fail the other.

The reward-scale trap, and why it belongs in your limitations section. Pendulum's per-step reward is roughly \([-16, 0]\), so \(\alpha = 0.5\) is a small temperature there and would be enormous on a task rewarded in \([0,1]\). SAC has no scale invariance: \(\alpha\) is compared directly against rewards inside the same backup. If you rescale rewards, normalise observations, or clip either, you have changed the effective temperature and any α you tuned before is stale. State the reward range you trained against in the model card, and say how you would test whether the limitation binds — the test is a rerun at a rescaled reward with α scaled by the same factor, checking that the learning curves coincide.
What carries forward. The bias–variance dial of §1 is the same dial the rollout depth exposes in Topic 5's tree search: a deeper simulation is a longer unbiased sample, and a learned evaluator is the critic that replaces it. The trust region of §3 returns in Topic 6 as the KL penalty that keeps a fine-tuned language model near its reference. And the identity of §6 — that \(r + \alpha\mathcal{H}(\pi)\) and \(r - \beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\) are one regularised objective with two different references — is the single equation Topic 6 is built on. When you meet \(\pi^\star \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}\exp(r/\beta)\) there, you will have already derived it here with \(\pi_{\text{ref}} = U\).
