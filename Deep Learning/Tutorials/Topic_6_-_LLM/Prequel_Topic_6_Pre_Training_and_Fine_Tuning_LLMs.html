Pre-training and fine-tuning in one idea: first go to school, then learn the job

In Topic 5 you built the transformer and met its three family members: the Reader (BERT), the Writer (GPT) and the Translator (T5). (Need a refresher? Meet the family again.) This page answers the next question in plain language: how do those models get smart, and how do you make one of them yours? The lecture and the sessions then add the math, the metrics and the code.

1Nobody hires a newborn

Suppose your team needs someone to sort incoming text messages into spam and not spam. You could raise a child from birth and teach them nothing but spam sorting. Or you could hire an adult who already reads English and show them a few hundred examples on their first morning.

Pre-training is the twelve years of school. Fine-tuning is the first week on the job.

A model trained from scratch on your small labeled set has to learn what words are, what grammar is, and what spam looks like, all from a few hundred examples. A pre-trained model arrives already knowing the language. It only has to learn your task.

2School without a teacher: four homework games

School for a language model has a problem: nobody can hand-label the internet. The trick is to make the text grade itself. Hide part of a sentence, ask the model to recover it, and check against the original. No human labels needed, which is why this is called self-supervised learning. There are four classic ways to play that game, and the game a model played in school decides what it is good at for life.

MLM · BERT

Fill in the blank. A cloze test. You may look left and right, so you become a careful reader: classification, search, tagging.

CLM · GPT

Finish my sentence. Your phone's autocomplete, taken seriously. You may only look left, so you become a fluent writer.

Span corruption · T5

Restore the redacted memo. Whole phrases are blacked out and you write them back. Text in, text out: a translator and summarizer.

Contrastive · SimCSE, CLIP

Find the twin. Decide which items mean the same thing. You never produce a word; you learn to shelve similar things together, like a librarian. This is where embeddings come from.

→ Session 1: you'll write all four games as code and compute each loss by hand.

3Fine-tuning: keep the brain, swap the hat

To put a graduate to work you don't rebuild their brain. You keep everything they learned and add a small, task-shaped piece on top, called a head. For spam sorting, the head is a tiny layer that turns the model's summary of the message into two numbers: spam and not spam.

In code this is one line: AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained("distilbert-base-uncased", num_labels=2). The library loads the schooled body and bolts on a fresh, untrained head. → Session 2: you'll write the training loop that the Hugging Face Trainer runs for you.

4Use a chisel, not a sledgehammer

A pre-trained model is a statue that is 95% carved. Fine-tuning finishes the face. If you swing a sledgehammer (a big learning rate, or far too many passes over a tiny dataset), you get your detail but smash the rest of the statue. The model gets better at your one task while losing the general knowledge that made it useful. That failure has a name: catastrophic forgetting.

This is why fine-tuning uses learning rates near 2e-5, about a hundred times smaller than you used when training from scratch, and only 2 to 5 epochs. It also starts with a short warmup: the learning rate ramps up from zero, the way you stretch before you sprint, because the brand-new head produces wild gradients in its first few steps.

5Sticky notes instead of reprinting the textbook

Updating every weight in a model with billions of them is like reprinting a whole textbook to fix a few pages. LoRA leaves the book untouched and adds sticky notes: two thin matrices whose product is the correction. Train only the notes. If you don't like the result, peel them off and the original model is still there.

6One exam score is one roll of the dice

Your fine-tuned model scores 87% on the test set and the zero-shot baseline scores 79%. Is that a real win? It depends on which 200 messages happened to land in the test set. Pick a different 200 and both numbers move.

You can't collect a thousand new test sets, but you can fake it honestly: draw 200 messages from your own test set, with repeats allowed, re-score, and do that many times. The spread of those scores is your uncertainty. That is the bootstrap, and the middle 95% of the spread is the 95% confidence interval.

Try it. Each click re-draws the test set 25 times and adds 25 dots per model. This demo runs a real bootstrap on a made-up test set of 200 messages.

If the two intervals overlap heavily, you have not shown a winner, and saying so is part of the job. Your FleetDeck leaderboard has to print the interval next to every metric. → Session 3.

7Rent a genius by the word, or train your own specialist?

A frontier model behind an API is a brilliant consultant who bills for every word read and written. A fine-tuned small model is an intern you trained yourself: narrower, but fast, private, and paid a flat rate. Which is cheaper depends almost entirely on volume.

Cost is only half the story. The consultant wins when you have no labeled examples, when the task keeps changing, or when it needs broad reasoning. The specialist wins on a narrow, stable, high-volume task where milliseconds and privacy matter. Your FineTuneFleet report has to argue this with numbers, not adjectives.

8Keep a lab notebook, and keep fingerprints instead of letters

Three months from now someone will ask, “Why did we ship this checkpoint?” If the answer lives in your memory, there is no answer. So every training run writes one row to an experiments table: learning rate, batch size, epochs, score, duration. That table is the lab notebook, and the leaderboard is just that table, sorted.

Every prediction is logged too, with one rule: never store the customer's text. Store its fingerprint, a SHA-256 hash. The same message always gives the same fingerprint, so you can count and audit, but nobody can read the message back out of it.

Try it. Type a message. Change one character and watch the whole fingerprint change.
