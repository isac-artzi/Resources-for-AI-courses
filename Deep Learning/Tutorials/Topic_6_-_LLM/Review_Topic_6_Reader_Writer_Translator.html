Meet the family again: the Reader, the Writer and the Translator

In Topic 5 you built one transformer block and saw that three famous model families are that same block with different rules. Before we ask how these models are trained, here is a quick tour of who they are, what each is good at, and how they work as a team.

1One block, three rules about who may look at whom

Every transformer is built from the same parts: attention (the words talk) and a small feed-forward network (each word thinks). The three family members differ in one thing only: which words each word is allowed to look at.

Reader

Encoder · BERT

Reads the whole sentence at once, left and right. Like a student who reads the full paragraph before answering a question about it.

Writer

Decoder · GPT

Sees only what has been written so far and adds the next word. Like writing a story with a card covering everything to the right of your pen.

Translator

Encoder–decoder · T5

A Reader and a Writer joined. The Writer keeps glancing back at what the Reader understood. Like an interpreter who listens to the full sentence, then speaks.

Try it. Pick a family member, then click a word to see which words it is allowed to look at.

2Who do you call for which job?

The rule each member lives by decides what it is naturally good at. Seeing both sides makes the Reader good at understanding. Seeing only the past makes the Writer good at continuing. Reading one text and writing another makes the Translator good at transforming.

Try it. Seven jobs arrive at the family business. Send each one to the member whose native talent fits best.

A fair caveat: today's very large Writers can also summarize and translate when you ask them in a prompt. “Native talent” here means the job each design does well at a small size and low cost, which is the comparison your products care about.

3How each one works: count the passes

A pass is one trip of the text through the whole stack of blocks. How many passes a member needs explains why your TrioFormer lanes had such different latencies.

Passes used
0

The Reader answers in one pass, however long the sentence is. The Writer needs one pass per new word. The Translator reads once, then writes one word per pass. That is why BERT was your fastest lane and why generation time grows with output length.

4How they work together

Notice the division of labor: the cheap, fast Reader touches every message; the slower Writer is called only when words have to be produced. Your Topic 6 product makes exactly this kind of choice: a small fine-tuned Reader for spam, or a small Translator for summaries.

5Where did each one get its talent?

Nobody programmed the Reader to understand or the Writer to write. Each learned its talent from a practice game it played billions of times before you ever downloaded it. That practice is called pre-training, and it is today's topic.

Reader

Practiced filling in blanks, looking both ways.

Writer

Practiced guessing the next word, looking only back.

Translator

Practiced restoring blacked-out phrases: damaged text in, missing text out.

There is also a fourth relative you have not met, the Librarian, who never writes a word and instead learns to shelve similar things together. You will meet all four games next.
