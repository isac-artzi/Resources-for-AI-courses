Why fine-tuning works, and where it quietly goes wrong

The lecture notes tell you what to do: mask 15%, use a learning rate near 2e-5, add LoRA, report a confidence interval. This page is about why each of those choices is what it is. Every demo below computes real numbers in your browser.

1Why 15%, and why 80 / 10 / 10?

BERT hides about 15% of the tokens. Hide far fewer and each sentence yields almost no practice problems. Hide far more and there is too little context left to solve them. But there is a second problem: the [MASK] token never appears when the model is actually used. A model that only ever practiced on [MASK] would learn “I only need to think hard when I see a blank.”

80% → [MASK]

The main game: recover the word from both sides.

10% → a random word

Now any word in the sentence might be wrong, so the model must check every word against its context.

10% → left unchanged

The model is still graded here, so it must build a good representation of ordinary, visible words, which is what fine-tuning will use.

The cost

Only 15% of positions teach anything per pass. A causal LM learns from every position. That is one reason BERT-style pre-training needs many passes over the corpus.

2Contrastive learning: the temperature decides who you argue with

InfoNCE is a multiple-choice question: “which of these N sentences is my twin?” The temperature τ divides every similarity before the softmax. A small τ stretches the differences, so the loss concentrates on the hard negatives: the wrong answers that look almost right. A large τ flattens everything, and every wrong answer gets pushed about equally.

Try it. One anchor, four candidates with fixed cosine similarities. Move τ.

SimCSE uses τ = 0.05. Its other trick is where the twin comes from: the same sentence is passed through the encoder twice, and dropout makes the two passes slightly different. That tiny difference is the only “augmentation.”

3Why LoRA works: the update has only a few real directions

LoRA bets that the change fine-tuning makes to a weight matrix, ΔW, is low-rank: a handful of directions carry nearly all of it. If that is true, two thin matrices can store the change. The singular value decomposition lets you test the bet. Sort the directions of ΔW by strength; keeping the strongest r is the best possible rank-r copy (the Eckart–Young theorem).

Slide the fade toward “slow” and the bet fails: every direction matters and a small r loses a lot. That is the honest limit of LoRA. It works well when the new task is close to what the model already knows, and less well when you are teaching it something truly new. In Exercise 4 you check the other half of the claim in code: whatever B and A learn, rank(B·A) ≤ r.

4Not every layer needs the same chisel

Lower layers of a transformer hold general knowledge (word forms, syntax). Upper layers hold more task-shaped features. So the layers that most need to change are at the top, and the layers most worth protecting are at the bottom. Four techniques follow from that one observation:

Freeze the body, train the head.

Cheapest and safest, with no forgetting at all. Often a few points worse, because the features can't adapt. A good first baseline row for your leaderboard.

Layer-wise learning-rate decay.

Top layer gets η, the next gets 0.9·η, then 0.81·η, and so on. The bottom barely moves.

Gradual unfreezing.

Train the head first, then unfreeze one more layer per epoch from the top down.

LoRA.

Freeze everything and add low-rank corrections, usually to the attention query and value matrices in every layer.

All four are answers to the same question from the prequel: how do I finish the statue's face without smashing the rest?

5Two honest intervals, and why pairing is sharper

The prequel ended on a cliffhanger: two models, two overlapping intervals, no clear winner. Here is the sharper test. Both models answered the same 200 messages. When a resample happens to be easy, both scores go up; when it is hard, both go down. That shared luck is noise you can cancel: on each resample, compute the difference and build the interval for that.

Try it. A real bootstrap with 2,000 resamples on a made-up test set. The zero-shot model makes every mistake the fine-tuned one makes, plus some more, which is typical.

In symbols: Var(A − B) = Var(A) + Var(B) − 2·Cov(A, B). Pairing keeps the covariance term, and that term is large when two models share most of their mistakes. Report the paired interval for Δ in your README. If it includes zero, say so.

6When the big model wins anyway

Discussion Question 2 asks for a case where zero-shot prompting a large model beats your fine-tuned small one. There are several, and a good engineer names them before the customer does:

  • No labels. Fine-tuning needs hundreds of labeled examples. If nobody has time to write them, there is nothing to fine-tune on.
  • The task keeps moving. New categories every month mean re-labeling and re-training every month. A prompt can be edited in a minute.
  • Low volume. Below the break-even point the API is simply cheaper, and you run no servers.
  • Broad reasoning or world knowledge. A 66-million-weight model cannot reason about a contract or a medical note the way a frontier model can.
  • Long, open-ended outputs. Small encoders classify. They do not write.

The middle path, few-shot prompting with a handful of examples in the prompt, often makes a strong baseline row for the leaderboard too.

7Three ways a fine-tune fools you (Discussion Question 4)

Contamination

The same or near-identical message sits in both train and test, so the test score measures memory. Safeguard: de-duplicate before splitting, split once with a fixed seed, and store the split label in the documents table.

Forgetting

The model aces your test set and falls apart on anything slightly different. Safeguard: keep a small general or adversarial check set, stop early, and prefer LoRA or a frozen body.

Privacy leakage

Models can memorize rare training strings, and logs can store customer text. Safeguard: scrub personal data before training, and log a SHA-256 hash of each request instead of the text.
