Transformers in one idea: every word looks at every other word, all at once

Last topic you watched RNNs and LSTMs read a sentence the way you read with a finger under each word, carrying a memory forward one step at a time. This page is the whole of Topic 5 in plain language. The lecture and the four in-class sessions then add the math, the shapes, and the code.

1The problem with passing notes down a line

An RNN is like a row of students passing a note. Word 1 writes something, hands it to word 2, who adds to it and hands it on. By word 40, the note has been rewritten 39 times. You saw what that does: early words fade (vanishing gradients) and nobody can start until the person before them is done, so a GPU sits mostly idle.

The transformer's move is simple to say: put everyone at a round table. Every word can talk to every other word directly, and all those conversations happen at the same time. Distance stops mattering, and the whole sentence is processed in one parallel step. That parallelism is why transformers could be trained on most of the internet.

2Attention: who should I listen to?

Each word asks a question, every word wears a name tag, and each word listens to the others in proportion to how well their name tag answers its question.

That sentence is attention. In the lecture the three parts get names:

Query

What am I looking for? (“I'm the word it; I need to know what I refer to.”)

Key

What do I offer? (“I'm animal: a living thing, a noun, something that can be tired.”)

Value

What do I actually pass along if you listen to me? (the meaning that gets mixed in)

Weights

Query·Key scores, squashed by softmax so they add up to 100%. Each word's new meaning = a weighted blend of everyone's Values.

Try it. Click a word to see where it “looks.” The orange bar under each word shows how much attention it gets. Then change the last word and watch it change its mind.

The weights here are illustrative, chosen to show the idea; a trained model learns its own. The famous version of this example comes from Google's 2017 Transformer blog post.

3Many heads: several conversations at once

One round of “who should I listen to?” can only chase one kind of relationship. So the transformer runs several small attentions side by side, called heads, each with its own question/name-tag/value projections. They split the same budget; they don't add parameters. What they add is specialization:

Head A · neighbors

“What word came right before me?”

Head B · grammar

“I'm a verb. Who is my subject?”

Head C · references

“I'm a pronoun. Who am I standing in for?”

Their answers are glued together and mixed. → Session 1 (Week 9 Wednesday): you'll build exactly this and check every shape.

4The catch: a round table has no “first seat”

If everyone talks to everyone at once, the model can't tell dog bites man from man bites dog. Same words, same conversations, same result, just shuffled. Attention on its own sees a set, not a sequence.

manseat 1
bitesseat 2
dogseat 3

Without seat numbers, shuffling changes nothing the model can see.

The fix is to stamp each word with its position before (or during) attention. There are three popular stamps: fixed sine waves (sinusoidal), a learned lookup table (learned), and rotating each word's question and name tag by an angle set by its position (rotary, used by most modern LLMs). → Session 2 (Week 9 Friday).

5Talk, then think, then repeat

A transformer block is two steps. First the words talk (attention: share information across the table). Then each word thinks on its own (a small feed-forward network, the same MLP you built in Topic 2). Stack 12, 24 or 96 of these blocks and you have BERT, GPT-2 or a modern LLM.

The highway matters: because each block adds to the running representation instead of replacing it, gradients have a clean path back through dozens of blocks. That's the same lesson as the LSTM's cell-state “conveyor belt” from Topic 4. → Session 3 (Week 10 Wednesday): you'll build the block and prove it matches PyTorch's own.

6One recipe, three family members

Change who may look at whom and you get the three transformer archetypes. Your TrioFormer product serves all three side by side.

Reader

Encoder · BERT

Every word sees the whole sentence, both directions. Trained to fill in blanks. Great at understanding: classify, search, fill a [MASK].

Writer

Decoder · GPT

Each word sees only the past (no peeking at the answer). Trained to guess the next word. Great at generating: continue, chat, code.

Translator

Encoder–decoder · T5

A reader and a writer joined: the writer keeps glancing back at what the reader understood. Great at transforming text: summarize, translate.

→ Session 4 (Week 10 Friday): masks, the KV cache that makes writing fast, and the temperature/top-p knobs behind every “creativity” slider.

7The price of the round table

Everyone-talks-to-everyone means the number of conversations grows with the square of the text length. Double the text and attention does about four times the work. That single fact explains context-window limits, why long prompts cost more, and the latency curve the Honors addendum asks you to measure on your own hardware.
