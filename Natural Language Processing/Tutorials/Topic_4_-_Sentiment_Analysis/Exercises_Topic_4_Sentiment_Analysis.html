1 · Precision, recall, F1 by hand math

By-hand computation

A sentiment classifier is evaluated on 200 held-out reviews. You are detecting negative reviews, so "negative" is the positive class. At the shipped threshold the model produces this confusion matrix:

                 predicted NEG    predicted POS
actually NEG          30               20
actually POS          10              140

Compute precision, recall, F1, and accuracy. Then answer: which single number would you put in a status report, and which one would you put in the model card?

Show solution

Read off the four counts: TP = 30 (actually negative, predicted negative), FN = 20, FP = 10, TN = 140.

precision = TP / (TP + FP) = 30 / (30 + 10) = 30 / 40  = 0.750
recall    = TP / (TP + FN) = 30 / (30 + 20) = 30 / 50  = 0.600

F1 = 2 · P · R / (P + R)
   = 2 · 0.750 · 0.600 / (0.750 + 0.600)
   = 0.900 / 1.350
   = 0.6667

  check with the shortcut form:
F1 = 2·TP / (2·TP + FP + FN) = 60 / (60 + 10 + 20) = 60 / 90 = 0.6667   ✓

accuracy = (TP + TN) / N = (30 + 140) / 200 = 170 / 200 = 0.850

Neither number alone is honest. Accuracy is 0.850 and is the most flattering, but only 50 of the 200 reviews are actually negative, so a model that always predicted "positive" would score 0.750 accuracy while catching nothing. The model card should carry all four numbers plus the threshold that produced them, because precision and recall are both functions of that threshold and the pair (0.750, 0.600) is the operating point the product actually experiences: four out of five flagged reviews really are negative, and two out of five negative reviews are missed.

2 · Lexicon score with negation and an intensifier mathinteractive

By-hand computation, then check

Use the scoring rules from the notes: base polarities good = +2, intensifier very = ×1.5 when it appears in the two tokens before the polarity word, negation ×(−0.75) when a negator appears in the three tokens before it. Intensifiers are applied first, then negation.

Compute the total score of:

"The service was not very good."

Then predict what happens to the score if you switch the negation rule off, and check both with the widget.

Widget · Lexicon scorer







Show solution

Tokenize: the · service · was · not · very · good. Only good is in the lexicon, with base +2.


base                                  = +2.00
intensifier: "very" is 1 token before → ×1.5   = +3.00
negation:    "not"  is 2 tokens before → ×-0.75 = -2.25

total = -2.25   ->   NEGATIVE


With the negation rule switched off the score is +3.00, i.e. more positive than the plain word "good" on its own, because the intensifier still fires and amplifies a polarity that has been inverted by a word the scorer is now ignoring. That is the worst kind of error: the rule that was supposed to add precision has multiplied a sign error.


Note the order matters. If you applied negation first and then the intensifier you would get 2 × (−0.75) × 1.5 = −2.25 — the same, because multiplication commutes. It stops commuting the moment a rule is additive or clipped, which is why real rule systems specify the order explicitly.






3 · Contrast: predict, then check interactive


Prediction-then-check


Consider the sentence:


"The food was great but the service ruined it."


Before touching the widget, write down three numbers: (a) the raw sum with every rule switched off, (b) the score with negation and intensifiers on but contrast off, (c) the score with contrast on, given the rule "polarity words before the marker are multiplied by 0.4, words after it by 1.6". Base polarities: great = +3, ruined = −3. Then check with the widget above and explain, in one sentence, why answer (b) is the dangerous one.




Show solution

(a) raw sum:          +3 + (-3)                    = 0.00
(b) negation + intens: no negator, no intensifier
                       so nothing changes           = 0.00
(c) contrast on:      0.4 × (+3)  +  1.6 × (-3)
                    = +1.20 + (-4.80)               = -3.60


(b) is the dangerous answer because it is not obviously broken. A score of exactly 0.00 on a review whose plain-English verdict is negative will be reported as "neutral" or, if your threshold is score > 0 → positive versus ≤ 0 → negative, as a coin flip decided by a floating-point tie. Add one more mildly positive word anywhere in the sentence and the same review flips to positive. A system that fails at exactly zero fails silently, and the dashboard downstream will read a stream of these as "customers are neutral about us" rather than "our service is losing customers who like the food".


The wider lesson: the contrast rule recovers this case only because English convention puts the verdict after but. It is a convention, not a grammar rule, and §3 of the notes shows a fronted concessive that defeats it.






4 · Build a bag-of-words collision interactive


Construction, then check


Write two sentences that (i) have identical unigram count vectors and (ii) a competent human reader would label with opposite overall sentiment. Do not reuse the food/service pair from the notes. Check your pair with the widget: the L1 distance must read exactly 0.


Then answer: does adding bigrams to the representation fix your pair? Does it fix the general problem?

Show solution

Any pair built by swapping the two clauses of a contrast works, because swapping reorders tokens without adding or removing any. The default pair in the widget is one:


A: "the screen is gorgeous but the battery is terrible"   -> negative overall
B: "the battery is terrible but the screen is gorgeous"   -> positive overall

shared multiset: the×2, is×2, screen, battery, gorgeous, terrible, but
L1 distance between count vectors = 0


Other constructions that work: comparatives with the arguments swapped ("the new one is better than the old one" / "the old one is better than the new one"), and attributions swapped between two entities.


Do bigrams fix it? For this pair, yes. A counts the bigrams gorgeous_but and but_the, B counts terrible_but and but_the, so the vectors differ and a bigram model can separate them. That is exactly why ngram_range=(1,2) is the standard setting for a TF-IDF baseline.


Does it fix the general problem? No. For any fixed n you can build a pair that agrees on every n-gram and differs in label — push the distinguishing material further apart than n tokens ("the screen, which I looked at for a long time before deciding, is gorgeous ..."). Meanwhile the feature space grows roughly as vocabularyn, so you run out of training data long before you run out of counterexamples. Order-blindness is a property of the representation, and only a representation that reads order (a sequence model) removes it.

6 · AUC by counting pairs math


By-hand computation


Using the same eight documents from problem 5, compute the AUC directly from its definition: the probability that a randomly chosen positive scores higher than a randomly chosen negative. Count the pairs; do not integrate anything. Then explain why the AUC does not change if you apply the transformation s' = s² to every score.




Show solution

Positives: 0.95, 0.88, 0.65, 0.44. Negatives: 0.72, 0.58, 0.31, 0.12. There are 4 × 4 = 16 pairs. Count the ones where the positive scores higher:


0.95  >  0.72, 0.58, 0.31, 0.12   ->  4
0.88  >  0.72, 0.58, 0.31, 0.12   ->  4
0.65  >        0.58, 0.31, 0.12   ->  3   (loses to 0.72)
0.44  >              0.31, 0.12   ->  2   (loses to 0.72 and 0.58)
                                     ----
                            total    13

AUC = 13 / 16 = 0.8125


(If any positive tied a negative it would count as 0.5 of a pair. There are no ties here.)


Why s' = s² changes nothing: squaring is strictly increasing on [0,1], so si > sj ⟺ si² > sj². Every one of the 16 pairwise comparisons has the same outcome, so the count is the same, so the AUC is the same. AUC is a property of the ranking only. This is exactly the property exploited in §12 of the notes: a model can be squashed or stretched into arbitrary overconfidence without its AUC moving by a thousandth, which is why a ranking metric can never detect a calibration failure.

Show solution

ECE = Σ_b (n_b / N) · | acc(b) − conf(b) |

high: (20/100) · |0.70 − 0.95| = 0.20 · 0.25 = 0.0500
mid : (30/100) · |0.60 − 0.75| = 0.30 · 0.15 = 0.0450
low : (50/100) · |0.52 − 0.55| = 0.50 · 0.03 = 0.0150
                                              ------
                                        ECE = 0.1100


Every bin sits below the diagonal, and the gap grows with confidence: the model is overconfident, and worst exactly where it is loudest. When it says 0.95 it is right 0.70 of the time, so a production rule of the form "auto-resolve when confidence > 0.9" would be wrong on nearly a third of the cases it silently closes.


The cheapest fix is temperature scaling: fit a single scalar T on a held-out validation split by minimizing cross-entropy on logits / T. It cannot change any predicted label (dividing all logits by a positive constant preserves the argmax), so accuracy and AUC are untouched; it only rescales confidence. A fitted T > 1 is the numerical confirmation that the model was overconfident. Re-fit it whenever the model or the input distribution changes.

Show solution

def slice_report(y_true, y_pred, groups):
    """Accuracy and support per slice, plus the overall number."""
    assert len(y_true) == len(y_pred) == len(groups)
    rows = {}
    for g in sorted(set(groups)):
        idx = [i for i, gg in enumerate(groups) if gg == g]
        correct = sum(1 for i in idx if y_true[i] == y_pred[i])
        rows[g] = {"n": len(idx), "acc": correct / len(idx)}
    total = sum(1 for i in range(len(y_true)) if y_true[i] == y_pred[i])
    rows["OVERALL"] = {"n": len(y_true), "acc": total / len(y_true)}
    return rows


Checking by hand: slice A is indices 0–3, where predictions are 1,1,0,0 against truths 1,1,0,0 — 4 of 4 correct, accuracy 1.0. Slice B is indices 4–7, where 0,1,1,0 meets 1,0,1,0 — 2 of 4 correct, accuracy 0.5. Overall 6 of 8 = 0.75.


The 2% question. Set the widget to slice A: n = 980, correct = 980, and slice B: n = 20, correct = 10. Overall accuracy is 990/1000 = 0.990. A model with no gap at all would score 1.000. The slice is failing half the time and the headline number moved by one point. One point is inside the range you would attribute to a different random seed, so no, you would not notice — not from the average. You only notice by computing the metric per slice, which is why a bias audit is a reporting discipline rather than an algorithm.


Two things to add before this function is fit for an audit: the count per slice (already there — a 20-document slice cannot support a confident claim either way) and a confidence interval, since with n = 20 the 95% interval on an accuracy of 0.5 is roughly ±0.22.






9 · Code: negation-aware lexicon scorer code


Code writing with stated expected output


Implement score(text) with this specification:


    
  
  • Lowercase the text, strip punctuation, split on whitespace.
    • 
  
  • Lexicon: {"fast": 2, "good": 2, "slow": -2, "bad": -2}.
    • 
  
  • Negators: {"not", "never", "no"}. If a negator occurs within the three tokens before a polarity word, multiply that word's contribution by -0.75 (apply at most one negator, the nearest).
    • 
  
  • Return the sum of contributions as a float.
    • 



Expected output:


score("The delivery was fast.")            ->  2.0
score("The delivery was not fast.")        -> -1.5
score("The delivery was not fast and the support was bad.")  -> -3.5
score("Nothing here is in the lexicon.")   ->  0.0




Show solution

import re

LEX = {"fast": 2, "good": 2, "slow": -2, "bad": -2}
NEGATORS = {"not", "never", "no"}
WINDOW = 3
NEG_FACTOR = -0.75

def tokenize(text):
    return re.sub(r"[^a-z0-9'\s]", " ", text.lower()).split()

def score(text):
    toks = tokenize(text)
    total = 0.0
    for i, tok in enumerate(toks):
        base = LEX.get(tok)
        if base is None:
            continue
        factor = 1.0
        for j in range(i - 1, max(-1, i - 1 - WINDOW), -1):   # nearest first
            if toks[j] in NEGATORS:
                factor = NEG_FACTOR
                break
        total += base * factor
    return total


Walking the third case: tokens are the delivery was not fast and the support was bad. fast (index 4) has not at index 3, one token back, so it contributes 2 × −0.75 = −1.5. bad (index 9) has was, support, the in its window — no negator — so it contributes −2.0. Total −3.5. ✓


Three properties worth noticing about your own implementation. It scans backwards from the polarity word so the nearest negator wins, which matters for "not really no good". It applies at most one negator, so double negation is not handled — "not never bad" is scored as if it had one negator, and if you want that case you need a parity rule. And it silently returns 0.0 for text containing no lexicon entry, which is indistinguishable from a genuinely neutral document; a production scorer should return the coverage count as well, so a caller can tell "neutral" from "I have nothing to say about this".






10 · Code: aspect attribution code


Code writing with stated expected output


Implement aspect_scores(text) that segments a review into clauses, assigns each clause to every aspect whose terms it mentions, and returns a dict of aspect to summed clause score.


    
  
  • Segment on . , ; ! ? and on the words but, however, though.
    • 
  
  • Aspect dictionary: {"display": ["screen", "display"], "battery": ["battery", "charge"], "price": ["price", "cost"]}.
    • 
  
  • Clause polarity: sum of {"gorgeous": 3, "terrible": -3, "reasonable": 2} over the clause's tokens.
    • 
  
  • Aspects with no mention must be absent from the returned dict — not present with value 0.
    • 



Expected output:


aspect_scores("The screen is gorgeous but the battery is terrible.")
  ->  {'display': 3.0, 'battery': -3.0}

aspect_scores("The price is reasonable.")
  ->  {'price': 2.0}

aspect_scores("It arrived on Tuesday.")
  ->  {}


Then answer in one or two sentences: why does the specification insist that an unmentioned aspect be absent rather than zero?




Show solution

import re

ASPECTS = {"display": ["screen", "display"],
           "battery": ["battery", "charge"],
           "price":   ["price", "cost"]}
TERM = {t: a for a, terms in ASPECTS.items() for t in terms}
LEX = {"gorgeous": 3, "terrible": -3, "reasonable": 2}

SPLIT = re.compile(r"[.,;!?]|\bbut\b|\bhowever\b|\bthough\b", re.I)

def tokenize(text):
    return re.sub(r"[^a-z0-9'\s]", " ", text.lower()).split()

def aspect_scores(text):
    out = {}
    for clause in SPLIT.split(text):
        toks = tokenize(clause)
        if not toks:
            continue
        polarity = float(sum(LEX.get(t, 0) for t in toks))
        found = {TERM[t] for t in toks if t in TERM}
        for a in found:
            out[a] = out.get(a, 0.0) + polarity
    return out


Trace of the first case: splitting on but and the final period gives the clauses "The screen is gorgeous" and "the battery is terrible". The first has polarity +3 and mentions screen → display; the second has polarity −3 and mentions battery. Result {'display': 3.0, 'battery': -3.0}. The third case has no aspect terms and no polarity terms, so the loop adds nothing and the dict stays empty. ✓


Why absent, not zero. Zero means "mentioned and neutral"; absence means "not discussed". If you conflate them, every average over an aspect is dragged toward zero by documents that had no opinion about it, so the reported aspect sentiment becomes a function of how often the aspect was mentioned rather than what was said about it. A product that never gets mentioned would appear to have neutral sentiment instead of no data, and the dashboard would quietly report a fact that nobody stated. Report the aspect score and the mention count together, always.






11 · Use limitation from a slice failure math


Explain why — measured failure to written limitation


You run a sliced evaluation of a deployed review classifier and get this table:


slice                          n       accuracy    macro-F1
  reviews  < 20 tokens       3,140       0.913        0.905
  reviews  >= 20 tokens      6,860       0.926        0.921
  contains a negator         1,470       0.781        0.762
  no negator                 8,530       0.945        0.939
  OVERALL                   10,000       0.922        0.916


(a) Name the mechanism behind the negator gap. (b) Write a use limitation that follows from it — a rule specific enough that an engineer could implement it and an auditor could check it. (c) State one thing this table does not tell you that you would need before you could call the audit complete.




Show solution

(a) Mechanism. Documents containing a negator score 16.4 accuracy points below documents without one (0.781 vs 0.945), and the gap is larger in macro-F1 than in accuracy, which means the errors are concentrated in the smaller class rather than spread evenly. The mechanism is compositional: negation inverts the polarity of a span, the scope of that inversion is syntactic, and both a bag-of-words baseline and a truncated encoder approximate scope by proximity. Whenever the negator is far from the word it scopes over — "this is not, by any stretch, a bad phone" — the inversion is missed and the model returns the polarity of the un-negated word, which is exactly the wrong sign rather than an uncertain answer. The length slices show a gap of only 1.3 points, which is small enough that it may well be noise at these sample sizes; the negator slice is the real finding.


(b) Use limitation. Something an engineer can implement and an auditor can check, for example:


"Documents matched by the negation detector are excluded from automated resolution. Any such document routed as negative must clear a calibrated confidence floor of 0.90 and be reviewed by a human before any customer-visible action is taken. The service's published accuracy figure must be accompanied by the per-slice table, and the negator slice figure must be quoted in any material that states an accuracy number. This restriction is reviewed whenever the model version changes."


Note what makes it enforceable: it names the trigger (a detector that already exists — it is the same regular expression that built the slice), it names the action (exclude from auto-resolution, require review), it names the threshold, and it names the review condition. "The model should be used with caution on negation" is not a limitation; it is a mood.


(c) What is missing. Several candidates, any one of which is a complete answer:


    
  
  • Confidence intervals. With n = 1,470 the interval on 0.781 is roughly ±0.02, so this gap is real — but the length gap of 1.3 points is not obviously outside noise, and the table gives no basis to distinguish the two.
    • 
  
  • Overlap between slices. Short reviews may contain proportionally more negators, in which case the two rows are measuring one phenomenon twice. A cross-tabulation would settle it, and the fix differs depending on the answer.
    • 
  
  • Error direction. Accuracy does not say whether negated documents fail toward false positives or false negatives, and the appropriate limitation differs: missing angry customers and libelling calm ones are different harms.
    • 
  
  • Confidence on the failing slice. If the model is also confident when wrong on this slice, a confidence floor will not catch the errors and part (b)'s limitation is ineffective.
    • 







12 · Use limitation from a calibration failure math


Explain why — measured failure to written limitation


A team reports these numbers for their fine-tuned sentiment model on a held-out split:


accuracy 0.906    macro-F1 0.899    ROC-AUC 0.961    ECE 0.147
predictions with confidence >= 0.95:  38% of the test set
observed accuracy within that group:  0.84


The product routes any prediction with confidence ≥ 0.95 straight into an automated action with no human in the loop. (a) Explain precisely why the excellent AUC is not evidence that this routing rule is safe. (b) Write the use limitation. (c) The team proposes "raise the confidence bar to 0.99". Say whether that fixes it and why.




Show solution

(a) Why AUC does not help. AUC measures ranking only: it is the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative. It is invariant under every strictly increasing transformation of the scores, so a model can have AUC 0.961 while reporting confidences that are arbitrarily wrong in magnitude. The routing rule does not use the ranking — it uses the number, as though it were a probability. The relevant measurement is the one they also reported and did not act on: within the group the model calls 0.95-or-better, it is right 0.84 of the time. That is roughly one wrong automated action in every six, taken silently, in exactly the population the rule was designed to fast-track. ECE of 0.147 says the same thing as a single scalar.


(b) Use limitation.


"Raw model confidence may not be used as a probability by any downstream rule. Automated action gated on confidence is permitted only after temperature scaling fitted on a held-out validation split, and only at a threshold whose observed accuracy on that split has been measured and recorded. The calibrated threshold and its measured accuracy are published in the model card, re-fitted on every model version, and re-checked on fresh samples on a fixed schedule, because temperature is a property of the model and of the input distribution together. Until that is done, every prediction, at any confidence, is advisory and requires human confirmation before an irreversible action."


(c) Does raising the bar to 0.99 fix it? No, and the reason matters. Raising the bar shrinks the automated population — fewer documents clear 0.99 than 0.95 — so the total number of bad automated actions falls, which will look like an improvement. But the model is overconfident, which means the mapping from reported confidence to observed accuracy is unknown and non-linear; there is no reason to believe the group above 0.99 is right 99% of the time, and with an ECE of 0.147 concentrated at the top of the range it may be barely better than the 0.95 group. Picking a number without measuring the accuracy at that number is the same mistake at a different value. The fix is to calibrate first, then choose the threshold from a measured reliability table, then re-measure. If after calibration the top bin still under-performs the product's requirement, the correct conclusion is that this decision cannot be automated at all.






13 · Defending an aspect inventory math


Explain why — modelling decision


Your team ships an aspect-based sentiment service for a food-delivery product with the inventory {food_quality, delivery_speed, price, app_experience}. Six months later, support is drowning in complaints about couriers entering buildings when nobody is home, and none of it appears anywhere in the aspect dashboards.


(a) Explain, mechanically, why the dashboard shows nothing. (b) Name two decisions in the original inventory design that produced this outcome. (c) Describe the process change that would have caught it, and state the cost of that change.




Show solution

(a) Mechanically. The aspect inventory is a closed list, and the attribution step assigns a clause to an aspect only when it matches that aspect's terms. Courier-conduct complaints match none of the four categories, so every such clause is dropped — either into an "other" bucket that nobody charts, or out of the pipeline entirely. The dashboards then report exactly what they were built to report, with no indicator anywhere that a growing share of input is falling outside the schema. This is the failure discussed in §9 of the notes: a category that does not exist produces no evidence, and no evidence is read by everyone downstream as no problem. The system is not wrong; it is answering a question nobody updated.


(b) Two design decisions. Any two of:


    
  
  • Closed inventory with no review schedule. Closed is a defensible choice — it gives comparable time series — but it is only safe when paired with a scheduled review that re-derives categories from recent data.
    • 
  
  • No coverage metric. Nothing measured the share of clauses that matched no aspect. That single number, charted over time, would have risen visibly months before support noticed. Coverage is the smoke alarm for a closed schema.
    • 
  
  • The inventory was derived from what the team already tracked (the four things the product roadmap cared about) rather than from a sample of real customer text, so it could only ever surface complaints the team had already thought of.
    • 
  
  • The inventory conflated the aspect with the metric: delivery_speed describes a timing attribute, so courier conduct has no home even though it is plainly about delivery.
    • 



(c) Process change and its cost. Add two things. First, a coverage metric: report, on every run, the fraction of clauses assigned to no aspect, and alert when it drifts upward. Second, a scheduled open-vocabulary pass — quarterly, cluster the unassigned clauses (embeddings plus a clustering step, or simply read a random sample of two hundred of them) and ask whether any cluster deserves a category.


The costs are real and should be stated rather than waved away. Adding a category breaks comparability: the time series before and after the change are not the same measurement, and every chart needs a version marker. Aspect-level metrics for a new category start with no labelled evaluation data, so its precision and recall are unknown until you annotate a sample, and reporting an aspect whose accuracy you have not measured is how a dashboard becomes confidently wrong. And a quarterly human read of unassigned text is recurring labour that has to be owned by a named person or it will not happen. That is the honest trade-off of a closed inventory: stable numbers, bought with a standing obligation to look at what the schema is throwing away.






14 · Sarcasm: diagnosis, fix, trade-off math


Open — design reasoning


Pick a domain you could plausibly build for (product reviews, support tickets, app-store feedback, internal survey text). Then:


    
  
  1. Give one realistic sarcastic or context-dependent example from that domain.
    2. 
  
  2. Explain why a fine-tuned transformer classifier — not just a lexicon — is likely to misclassify it.
    3. 
  
  3. Propose one specific improvement.
    4. 
  
  4. State one concrete trade-off your improvement introduces.
    5. 
  
  5. State the use limitation you would keep in place even after the improvement ships.
    6. 





Show solution

A complete answer has all five parts and is specific at every one. Here is one worked version; yours should differ in domain and detail.


1. Domain and example. App-store feedback for a note-taking app: "Love the new sync feature. Only took three tries and a support ticket to get my notes back." The human verdict is clearly negative.


2. Why a fine-tuned transformer still fails. The classifier learns the mapping from surface text to labels that its training data exhibits, and "love the new X" appears in that data overwhelmingly in genuinely positive reviews. Attention lets the model condition on the second sentence, but the second sentence contains no polarity vocabulary either — its negativity is an inference from a fact about the world (recovering your own notes should not take three tries and a ticket). Nothing in the training distribution teaches that inference unless sarcastic examples were labelled and present in quantity, and they are rare and expensive to label because annotators disagree about them. Worse, the failure is confident: cross-entropy training pushes the model toward extreme probabilities, so the wrong label arrives with a high number attached and sails through any confidence gate. That combination — rare in training, confident when wrong — is what makes sarcasm a deployment problem rather than an accuracy problem.


3. Improvement. Continue fine-tuning on a targeted set: mine candidates with cheap high-recall heuristics (a positive polarity word within one sentence of an explicit failure or effort term — "only took", "finally", "yet again", a numeral followed by "tries" or "hours"), have a human label a few thousand of them, and add them to training with class weighting so they are not swamped. Ship it with an "ironic" flag from the same detector, exposed in the API response.


4. Trade-off. The heuristic mining is high-recall and low-precision, so the human labelling budget is largely spent on non-sarcastic text — the cost is annotator time, and annotator agreement on irony is low enough that a share of the labels you buy will be noise. Upweighting a rare class also raises the false-positive rate on ordinary enthusiastic reviews: a genuinely happy customer who writes "Love it. Only took a minute to set up" starts getting flagged. You are trading precision on the majority of your traffic for recall on a small, high-harm slice. Whether that is worth it depends on the cost of the two errors, and you should measure both before and after rather than reporting only the improvement.


5. The limitation that survives the fix. The improvement moves an accuracy number; it does not change the nature of the failure, because irony is defined by a mismatch between what is said and what the reader knows, and no fixed training set covers what readers know. So the limitation stays:


"No consequential action affecting an individual customer, account, or employee may be taken on the basis of a single document's sentiment label. Documents flagged as ironic are routed to human review regardless of confidence. Model output is used for aggregate trends and for prioritizing human attention; it is never used as evidence about a person's intent, motives, or character, because the model measures the polarity of text under one annotation scheme and observes none of those things."


The general shape to reproduce, whatever domain you chose: a measured failure, the mechanism that causes it, a fix scoped to that mechanism, an honest account of what the fix costs, and a limitation that holds because the mechanism has not gone away.
