Topic 4 — Sentiment Analysis and Aspect-Based Sentiment

From counting polarity words to fine-tuned transformer classifiers, then down one level to aspects — and then the part that decides whether the system is safe to ship: measuring where it fails and writing down what it may not be used for.

What you should walk away with. (1) Able to state sentiment analysis as a supervised classification problem, including what the label space is and who chose it. (2) Able to compute a lexicon score by hand and to name four linguistic phenomena that defeat it. (3) Able to explain why a bag-of-words model cannot in principle separate certain sentence pairs. (4) Fluent with the mechanics of fine-tuning an encoder classifier and with the TF-IDF baseline it must beat. (5) Able to define an aspect inventory, defend it, and document it as a modelling decision. (6) Able to compute precision, recall, F1, macro-F1, and ECE by hand from a confusion matrix or a calibration table, and to read an ROC curve. (7) Able to run a sliced evaluation and explain why an average hides a subgroup failure. (8) Able to turn a measured failure into a written use limitation.

1 · Sentiment as a classification task math

Sentiment analysis is usually posed as supervised text classification. Given a document x (a review, a post, a support ticket), predict a label y from a fixed set. The standard formulation is:

ŷ = argmaxy ∈ Y P(y | x)

Everything interesting hides in three places that the equation does not show.

The label space is a choice, not a fact

Common choices, each of which changes the task:

  • Binary — {negative, positive}. The most common setup because it is the easiest to label and the easiest to source (star ratings collapse to it). It has no way to express "this text has no opinion in it", so every neutral document is forced into one of two wrong bins.
  • Ternary — {negative, neutral, positive}. More honest, but "neutral" is a heterogeneous class: it contains objective statements, mixed opinions, and off-topic text, and annotators agree on it far less than on the poles.
  • Ordinal — 1 to 5 stars. Now the classes are ordered, so confusing 1 with 2 is a smaller error than confusing 1 with 5, and plain accuracy is the wrong metric.
  • Multi-label emotion — joy, anger, fear, sadness, surprise, disgust. A document can carry several at once.
  • Aspect-based — a sentiment label per aspect of the entity, which is where these notes are headed from §8 onward.

The label is a property of a reader, not of the string

"The battery lasts about three hours" carries no polarity words at all. Whether it is negative depends on whether the product is a laptop (bad), a drone (fine), or a hearing aid (very good). The training label records what one annotator, under one set of guidelines, decided. Two consequences follow immediately, and both matter for the audit you will write later: inter-annotator agreement puts a ceiling on achievable accuracy, and any systematic tendency in the annotator pool becomes a systematic tendency in the model.

Why the task is harder than it looks

PhenomenonExampleWhat breaks
Negation"The food was not great."Polarity word present, polarity inverted.
Intensification / downtoning"Barely acceptable" vs "absolutely perfect"Magnitude carried by a neighbouring word.
Contrast"The food was great but the service ruined it."Opposite polarities; the second clause dominates the verdict.

Each row is a concrete engineering risk, not a curiosity. By the end of these notes you will have measured several of them and written a use limitation for each.

2 · Lexicon methods, computed mathinteractive

A sentiment lexicon is a dictionary mapping words to polarity values. The simplest scorer sums them:

score(x) = Σw ∈ x polarity(w)

Real rule-based scorers (the VADER family is the usual reference implementation, github.com/cjhutto/vaderSentiment) add hand-written rules on top: a negation window that flips and damps polarity, multiplicative intensifier factors, punctuation and capitalization boosts, and a contrast rule that downweights whatever comes before but. The widget below implements three of those rules so you can switch each one on and off over your own sentence.

The rules, precisely as implemented here, are:

  • Intensifier: if an intensifier appears in the two tokens before a polarity word, multiply by its factor (very 1.5, extremely 1.8, slightly 0.6, and so on).
  • Negation: if a negator appears in the three tokens before a polarity word, multiply by −0.75. The damping factor encodes the observation that "not great" is milder than "terrible".
  • Contrast: if the sentence contains but, however, or yet, multiply every polarity word before the marker by 0.4 and every one after it by 1.6.

Demo 1 · Lexicon scorer with switchable rules

Type a sentence. Each bar is one token's contribution; the red line is the running sum. Switch the rules on and off and watch the same sentence change verdict.







  Read the presets in order. "The food was great." scores +3 and is right. "The food was not great." scores +3 with the rules off — the true label flipped and the score did not move at all. Switch negation on and it becomes −2.25. Now try "great BUT ruined": with negation and intensifiers on but contrast off, the two polarity words cancel to exactly 0.00, and a system that thresholds at 0 will call a plainly negative review neutral or positive. Switch contrast on and it drops to −3.60. Finally try the sarcasm preset: no rule in the family fixes it, because the surface polarity is genuinely positive and only the pragmatics are negative.



Where lexicon methods are still the right call


They are not obsolete. A lexicon needs no training data, runs in microseconds, is fully inspectable (you can point at the exact word that produced the score), and cannot be poisoned by a skewed training set. That makes it a legitimate choice for a first-pass triage filter, for a sanity check on a learned model, and for the "explain this prediction" panel next to a neural score. What it cannot do is compose meaning.




3 · Four ways a lexicon breaks math



Negation is not a window


The ±3 window is a hack that works often enough to be useful and fails in a way you can characterize. Consider "This is not, by any stretch, a bad phone." The negator not sits five tokens before bad, outside the window, so bad contributes −2 unmodified and the scorer calls a positive review negative. Widening the window does not fix it: a wider window starts negating words that the negator does not scope over ("I did not go, and the food was great"). Negation scope is a syntactic property; approximating it with a distance threshold trades one error for another.



Intensifiers are multiplicative and unbounded in composition


"really very extremely good" stacks three factors. Rule systems cap the product to keep scores in range, which means the cap, not the language, decides the score. Downtoners are worse: "barely acceptable" is negative overall, but multiplying a positive base by a positive factor less than one can never cross zero, so a purely multiplicative scorer will never call it negative. You need a rule that says a downtoner below some threshold inverts rather than shrinks — another hand-tuned constant.



Contrast redistributes weight, and the marker's position matters


The but rule works because English discourse convention puts the speaker's actual verdict after the contrast marker. That convention is not universal within English. Fronted concessives invert it: "Although the food was great, the service ruined it" has the same verdict as the but version, but the concessive clause now comes first. Type that sentence into Demo 1 with every rule switched on: the score is 0.00, a neutral verdict for a plainly negative review, because the marker set is but, however, yet and none of them appears. Adding although to the marker set does not rescue it either — a fronted marker sits at position 0, so both clauses count as "after" it, both get the same weight, and the two polarity words cancel to 0.00 again. Getting fronted concessives right needs the clause structure, not the marker's position.



Sarcasm inverts polarity with no surface signal


"Oh good, another update that breaks everything." Every polarity-bearing token in that sentence is positive. The negativity lives in the mismatch between the stated evaluation and a fact the reader knows, and there is no lexical feature that marks it. Cues that sometimes help — an interjection followed by a positive word, an exclamation after a complaint, scare quotes, a hashtag — are weak, domain-specific, and heavily platform-dependent. This is the failure mode you will document first, because it is the one that survives every fix you can make with rules.




  The pattern to notice. Negation, intensification, and contrast are compositional failures: the meaning of the whole is a function of the parts and their arrangement, and a sum over words discards the arrangement. Sarcasm is a pragmatic failure: the meaning of the whole is not a function of the words at all. A model with context (a transformer) fixes a lot of the first kind. It fixes much less of the second.





4 · Why bag-of-words cannot win mathinteractive


A bag-of-words or TF-IDF representation maps a document to a vector of term counts (or weighted counts). Word order is discarded by construction. That is not a quality problem you can fix with more data or a better classifier — it is an information-theoretic limit. If two documents map to the same vector, then every classifier built on top of that representation, of any capacity, must assign them the same prediction.


So all you need to prove a bag-of-words sentiment model is unfixable is one pair of sentences with identical count vectors and different labels. Here is that pair:


A: "the service was good but the food was terrible"   → verdict: negative
B: "the food was terrible but the service was good"   → verdict: positive


Both contain the×2, was×2, service, food, good, terrible, but. The L1 distance between the count vectors is exactly zero. The widget below computes the vectors so you can confirm it, and lets you try to build your own colliding pair.

Adding bigrams to the representation patches this specific pair — but_the vs terrible_but now differ — and it genuinely helps with local negation, which is why ngram_range=(1,2) is a standard setting for a TF-IDF baseline. It does not solve the general problem: for any fixed n you can construct a pair that collides on all n-grams and differs in label, and the feature space grows fast enough that you run out of training data long before you run out of counterexamples.

6 · Fine-tuned transformer classifiers code


The modern default for document-level sentiment is an encoder-only transformer with a classification head, fine-tuned on labelled data. The architecture you need is exactly the encoder stack: bidirectional self-attention, a pooled representation, and a linear layer to the label space.



from datasets import load_dataset
from transformers import (AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForSequenceClassification,
                          TrainingArguments, Trainer)
import numpy as np

raw = load_dataset("imdb")                      # 25k train / 25k test, binary
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("distilbert-base-uncased")

def encode(batch):
    return tok(batch["text"], truncation=True, max_length=256)

ds = raw.map(encode, batched=True)

model = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(
    "distilbert-base-uncased", num_labels=2,
    id2label={0: "negative", 1: "positive"},
    label2id={"negative": 0, "positive": 1})

args = TrainingArguments(
    output_dir="runs/sentiment",
    learning_rate=2e-5,          # small: you are nudging pretrained weights
    per_device_train_batch_size=16,
    num_train_epochs=2,          # more than 3 usually overfits this size of set
    eval_strategy="epoch",       # older versions: evaluation_strategy
    load_best_model_at_end=True)

trainer = Trainer(model=model, args=args,
                  train_dataset=ds["train"], eval_dataset=ds["test"],
                  processing_class=tok, compute_metrics=metrics)
trainer.train()



The dataset is Maas et al.'s large movie review corpus (ai.stanford.edu/~amaas/data/sentiment; cite Maas, Daly, Pham, Huang, Ng & Potts, 2011, aclanthology.org/P11-1015). The Trainer walkthrough in the Hugging Face course is at huggingface.co/learn/llm-course/en/chapter3/3.



The baseline you must beat


Never report a transformer number without a linear baseline next to it. TF-IDF plus logistic regression trains in seconds on a laptop, and on clean single-domain review data it lands close enough to a fine-tuned encoder that the gap is the entire argument for the extra cost.



from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

baseline = make_pipeline(
    TfidfVectorizer(ngram_range=(1, 2), min_df=2, sublinear_tf=True),
    LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000, C=4.0))
baseline.fit(train_texts, train_labels)



The comparison is not only about a headline number. Report both models on the same held-out split, with the same metrics, and then find the input types where the gap is largest. In practice the transformer's advantage concentrates in exactly the categories from §3 and §5: sentences with negation at distance, sentences with a contrast marker, and long reviews whose verdict is stated once at the end. Those three categories, with one worked example each, are a far more useful result than a single accuracy difference, because they tell you when the extra serving cost is buying you something.

7 · What fine-tuning actually changes math


Mechanically, four things happen when you call AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(..., num_labels=2):


    
  
  1. The pretrained encoder weights are loaded. These already encode a great deal about which words go together.
    2. 
  
  2. A new, randomly initialized linear head of shape (d_model, num_labels) is attached on top of the pooled representation (for BERT-family models, the hidden state of the [CLS] position after a pooling layer). It knows nothing yet — this is why you see a warning about newly initialized weights, and why that warning is expected rather than a bug.
    3. 
  
  3. Logits z go through a softmax to produce class probabilities.
    4. 
  
  4. Training minimizes cross-entropy over the whole network, head and encoder together, at a small learning rate.
    5. 




  P(y = k | x) = exp(zk) / Σj exp(zj)   ·  
  L = − (1/N) Σi log P(yi | xi)



Three practical consequences that people get wrong:


    
  
  • The softmax output is not a probability you have earned. It is a normalized exponential of two numbers. It becomes a usable probability only after you check calibration (§12), and modern networks trained to convergence with cross-entropy are systematically overconfident.
    • 
  
  • Label order is a config field, not a convention. Set id2label and label2id explicitly. A silently inverted label map produces a model whose accuracy is exactly one minus the truth, and every downstream metric will look plausible.
    • 
  
  • Truncation is a modelling decision. max_length=256 on movie reviews cuts a meaningful share of documents. If the verdict is in the last paragraph — which it often is — you have thrown it away. Measure the accuracy difference between truncated and full-length documents; that difference is a slice result and belongs in your audit.
    • 




# Getting probabilities out, correctly
import torch
enc = tok(text, return_tensors="pt", truncation=True, max_length=256)
with torch.no_grad():
    logits = model(**enc).logits            # shape (1, num_labels)
probs = torch.softmax(logits, dim=-1)[0]    # shape (num_labels,)
pred  = int(probs.argmax())
print(model.config.id2label[pred], float(probs[pred]))




8 · Aspect-based sentiment interactive


A single label per document destroys the information a product team actually wants. "The pizza was delicious but the service was rude" is not a 3-star review to be averaged into a dashboard — it is a kitchen that works and a front-of-house that does not. Aspect-based sentiment analysis (ABSA) attributes sentiment to specific aspects of an entity rather than to the whole text.



The task decomposes into sub-tasks that you should keep separate in your head and in your code:


    
  
  • Aspect term extraction — find the spans that name an aspect ("pizza", "battery life", "checkout"). A token-level tagging problem.
    • 
  
  • Aspect category detection — map spans (or the whole sentence) to a closed inventory of categories such as food, service, price, ambience. A multi-label classification problem.
    • 
  
  • Aspect polarity classification — given the text and a specific aspect, predict the sentiment toward that aspect. This is the part where a transformer is genuinely better than anything simpler, because it can condition on the aspect: the pair (sentence, aspect) is fed in together, often as a sentence pair, and attention resolves which clause is about which aspect.
    • 



The SemEval-2014 Task 4 shared task (Pontiki et al.) established this decomposition and the laptop and restaurant datasets that most later ABSA work uses; see aclanthology.org/S14-2004.



Three implementation strategies



  
StrategyHow it worksWhen it is the right callWhat it costs you

  
Dictionary + clause segmentationSplit into clauses, match aspect terms from a hand-built list, score each clauseFixed narrow domain, no labelled aspect data, need full transparencyMisses paraphrases and implicit aspects; recall is bounded by your word list

  
Aspect-conditioned classifierFeed (text, aspect) as a sentence pair to a fine-tuned encoder; one forward pass per aspectYou have a closed aspect inventory and some labelled dataCost is linear in the number of aspects; still needs the inventory

  
Span tagging + polarityBIO-tag aspect terms, then classify polarity per spanOpen aspect vocabulary; you want to discover aspects you did not anticipateNeeds span-level annotation, which is expensive and low-agreement





The widget implements the first strategy end to end, because it is the one you can inspect at every step. Clauses are cut at sentence punctuation, commas, and the discourse markers but, however, though, although. Each clause is matched against an aspect dictionary and scored with the negation-aware lexicon from §2.

Edit the review. Each clause is assigned to zero or more aspects and scored independently; the bars are per-aspect totals and the dashed line is the document-level total.

  
  
  

  






  Read the document total against the aspect breakdown. On the default review the document-level score is positive, because one enthusiastic clause about food outweighs two complaints. A single-label system would file this review under "satisfied customer". The aspect breakdown says the opposite where it counts: service and price are negative, and those are the two things the business can act on. That difference in decision — not a metric — is the business case for ABSA.




The same widget shows the method's limits honestly. The clause "our order took forty minutes" contains no polarity word, so it scores 0 and contributes nothing, even though it is the strongest complaint in the review. Implicit sentiment is invisible to a lexicon. And "quiet" is not in the polarity dictionary at all, so the ambience score comes entirely from "clean" — a reminder that with a dictionary method, coverage is the model.




9 · Defining aspects is a modelling decision math


There is no aspect inventory in the data. You choose it, and the choice determines what the system can ever report. Treat it like a schema decision: write it down, version it, and justify every entry. At minimum, document these six decisions.



    
  
  1. Closed or open inventory. A closed list (food, service, price, ambience) gives you stable dashboards and comparable results across time. An open one discovers aspects you did not anticipate — which is the whole point of listening to customers — but the categories drift between runs and you cannot chart them. Most production systems pick closed and schedule a review.
    2. 
  
  2. Granularity. Is price one aspect or three (sticker price, value for money, hidden fees)? Finer categories give more actionable results and less data per category, which means worse per-aspect metrics and wider confidence intervals. State the granularity and the reason for it.
    3. 
  
  3. The absent-aspect case. If a review never mentions price, does the system emit null, neutral, or nothing at all? This is not cosmetic: if absence is coded as neutral, every average over the aspect is pulled toward neutral by documents that had no opinion, and the reported trend is a function of how many people mentioned it rather than what they said. Emit null, and report coverage (how many documents mentioned the aspect) alongside every aspect score.
    4. 
  
  4. Mentioned-but-neutral. "The room was about what you expect" mentions ambience with no polarity. That is a real neutral, distinct from absence, and it needs its own label.
    5. 
  
  5. Overlap and multi-assignment. "Overpriced for what you get" is both price and value; "the app crashed during checkout" is both reliability and checkout. Decide whether a clause may be assigned to several aspects, and say so — the widget in §8 assigns to every matching aspect, which inflates the number of aspect-level observations relative to a first-match-only rule.
    6. 
  
  6. Provenance. Who chose the inventory, from what evidence, and when is it reviewed? An inventory drawn from what the product team already believes is wrong will report on the wrong things very reliably, and will never surface the complaint you did not know about.
    7. 





  Why this is a fairness issue, not only a design issue. The aspect inventory decides what counts as feedback. If the inventory has no category for accessibility, then every complaint about accessibility falls into "other", is never charted, and is invisible to the people who decide what to fix. Absence of a category produces absence of evidence, and absence of evidence gets read as absence of a problem. Document the inventory and its review schedule as part of the model card.





10 · Confusion matrix, precision, recall, F1 mathinteractive


A binary classifier that outputs a score s ∈ [0,1] becomes a decision only when you pick a threshold t and predict positive when s ≥ t. Every metric below is a function of that threshold, which is why reporting a single accuracy number without stating the threshold is meaningless.




  
predicted positivepredicted negative

  
actually positiveTP (true positive)FN (false negative)

  
actually negativeFP (false positive)TN (true negative)






  precision = TP / (TP + FP)   ·   recall = TP / (TP + FN)

  F1 = 2 · precision · recall / (precision + recall) = 2·TP / (2·TP + FP + FN)

  accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN)




Worked example — compute it by hand before you trust the library


Take a held-out set of 200 reviews, of which 60 are genuinely negative (we treat "negative" as the positive class, because negative reviews are what the product team wants routed). At threshold t = 0.5 the model produces:


                  predicted NEG    predicted POS
actually NEG            41               19          (60 actually negative)
actually POS             9              131          (140 actually positive)


So TP = 41, FP = 9, FN = 19, TN = 131. Step by step:


precision = TP / (TP + FP)
          = 41 / (41 + 9)   = 41 / 50   = 0.820

recall    = TP / (TP + FN)
          = 41 / (41 + 19)  = 41 / 60   = 0.6833

F1        = 2 · P · R / (P + R)
          = 2 · 0.820 · 0.6833 / (0.820 + 0.6833)
          = 1.12067 / 1.50333
          = 0.7455

  check, via the shortcut form:
F1        = 2·TP / (2·TP + FP + FN)
          = 82 / (82 + 9 + 19) = 82 / 110 = 0.7455   ✓

accuracy  = (41 + 131) / 200 = 172 / 200 = 0.860


Read those four numbers as a sentence: when the system flags a review as negative it is right 82% of the time, but it only catches 68% of the negative reviews there are; overall it is right on 86% of documents. Accuracy is the highest of the four and the least informative, because 70% of the set is positive — a model that predicted "positive" for everything would score 0.70 accuracy with a recall of exactly zero.

Push the threshold to 0.90: precision climbs and recall collapses. Push it to 0.10: recall approaches 1.0 and precision falls toward the base rate. There is no threshold that is correct in the abstract. The threshold is a policy decision that encodes the relative cost of the two errors, and it belongs in the documentation next to the metric it produces. For a system that routes angry customers to a human, a false negative (an angry customer ignored) usually costs more than a false positive (a calm customer gets a phone call), so you pick a low threshold and accept the precision hit — and you say so in writing.




11 · ROC and AUC mathinteractive


An ROC curve removes the threshold from the picture by sweeping it. For every threshold, plot:



  TPR (recall, sensitivity) = TP / (TP + FN)   on the y-axis

  FPR = FP / (FP + TN)   on the x-axis



The curve starts at (1,1) with the threshold at 0 (everything predicted positive) and ends at (0,0) with the threshold at 1. The area under it, AUC, has an exact and useful interpretation: AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen positive example receives a higher score than a randomly chosen negative example. A perfect ranker scores 1.0, random scores 0.5. Because AUC depends only on the ordering of scores, it is invariant to any monotone transformation of them — a fact you will use in the next section to show that a model can have an excellent AUC and still lie about its confidence.




  
Demo 6 · Trace the ROC with the same threshold

  
Same 300 documents as Demo 5, and the same threshold — moving either slider moves both. The dot on the curve is the operating point you selected; the shaded region is the area being measured.

  

    

      

    

  

  





  AUC's blind spot. With heavy class imbalance — say 2% of tickets are abusive — a model can post an impressive AUC while its precision at any usable threshold is terrible, because the false-positive rate is computed against a huge negative pool. When the positive class is rare, report the precision–recall curve and average precision alongside ROC-AUC, and always report precision and recall at the threshold you actually ship.





12 · Calibration mathinteractive


A model is calibrated if, among all the predictions it makes with confidence 0.8, about 80% are correct. This is a completely separate property from accuracy or AUC, and it is the property you need whenever a downstream system uses the number rather than the label: routing rules, abstention thresholds, weighted averages, human-review triggers.


The standard diagnostic is a reliability diagram. Bin the predictions by confidence, and for each bin plot mean predicted confidence against the observed fraction correct. A calibrated model sits on the diagonal. A curve that falls below the diagonal in the high-confidence bins means the model claims more confidence than it earns: it says 0.95 and is right 0.84 of the time. The scalar summary is expected calibration error:



  ECE = Σb (nb / N) · | acc(b) − conf(b) |



Guo et al., "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks" (arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599), documents the central empirical fact: modern networks are systematically overconfident, and the effect grows with capacity and with training past the point where accuracy stops improving.




  
Demo 7 · Reliability diagram — overconfident vs calibrated

  
800 predictions. The sharpening slider applies a monotone transform to the model's probabilities. At 1.0 the model reports its true belief; above 1.0 it pushes every prediction toward 0 or 1 without changing the ranking. Watch AUC stay fixed while ECE and Brier score climb.

  

    

      

    
    

  

  
  





The invariance is the lesson. Sharpening leaves the ordering of scores untouched, so AUC does not move by a thousandth, while ECE more than triples. Any evaluation that reports only ranking metrics is blind to this failure, and the failure is exactly the one that breaks a production rule of the form "auto-resolve tickets when confidence > 0.95".



Fixing it: temperature scaling


The cheapest effective fix divides the logits by a single scalar T fitted on a held-out validation split by minimizing negative log-likelihood. It cannot change any prediction (dividing all logits by a positive constant preserves the argmax), so accuracy and AUC are untouched; it only rescales confidence.


import torch, torch.nn.functional as F

T = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.ones(1) * 1.0)
opt = torch.optim.LBFGS([T], lr=0.01, max_iter=100)

def closure():
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss = F.cross_entropy(val_logits / T, val_labels)   # val split only
    loss.backward()
    return loss

opt.step(closure)
print("fitted temperature:", float(T))     # > 1 means the model was overconfident
calibrated = torch.softmax(test_logits / T.detach(), dim=-1)


Fit T on validation data, never on test. Fitting the temperature on the same split you report ECE on gives you a flattering number that will not survive contact with new data. And re-fit after any change to the model or a shift in the input distribution: temperature is a property of the model and the data it sees.




13 · Macro vs micro averaging mathinteractive


With three or more classes you must choose how to average per-class metrics, and the choice changes the story.


    
  
  • Micro-average: pool all TP, FP, FN across classes and compute once. Every document counts equally. For single-label multi-class problems micro-F1 equals accuracy exactly.
    • 
  
  • Macro-average: compute the metric per class, then take the unweighted mean. Every class counts equally, so a small class that the model handles badly drags the number down.
    • 
  
  • Weighted average: per-class metrics weighted by class support. Close to micro; hides small-class failure the same way.
    • 



Report macro-F1 for sentiment. The neutral class is nearly always both the smallest and the hardest, and micro-averaging lets a model that never once predicts neutral post a respectable score.

14 · Sliced evaluation as a bias audit interactive


A bias audit is not a separate technique with its own theory. Mechanically, it is one thing: compute your existing metrics separately on subsets of the evaluation data, and compare. The subsets are called slices, and choosing them is where the judgment lives.



def slice_report(y_true, y_pred, groups):
    """Accuracy and support per slice, plus the overall number."""
    rows = {}
    for g in sorted(set(groups)):
        idx = [i for i, gg in enumerate(groups) if gg == g]
        correct = sum(1 for i in idx if y_true[i] == y_pred[i])
        rows[g] = {"n": len(idx), "acc": correct / len(idx)}
    total = sum(1 for i in range(len(y_true)) if y_true[i] == y_pred[i])
    rows["OVERALL"] = {"n": len(y_true), "acc": total / len(y_true)}
    return rows



Slices worth computing for a sentiment system, roughly in order of how often they surface something:


    
  
  • Document length — short documents have less evidence; long ones get truncated. Both fail, for opposite reasons.
    • 
  
  • Source or domain — movie reviews vs support tickets vs social posts. The most reliable way to find a large gap.
    • 
  
  • Product category or genre — the domain-specific polarity problem from §1 lives here.
    • 
  
  • Presence of a linguistic phenomenon — documents containing a negator, a contrast marker, an emoji, a question mark. Cheap to compute with a regular expression and directly tied to §3.
    • 
  
  • Language variety and code-switching — non-standard varieties, transliteration, mixed-language text.
    • 
  
  • Demographic proxies where you have consent to collect them — and only then; inferring protected attributes in order to audit for bias about them creates a new harm on top of the one you were measuring.
    •

  What to do about a gap you find. In order: (1) confirm it is real by checking the slice's sample size and computing a confidence interval — a 40-document slice can produce a 20-point gap from noise alone; (2) look at the errors themselves and name the mechanism, because "the model is worse on short documents" and "the model is worse on documents containing negation, and short documents contain proportionally more negation" imply different fixes; (3) fix what you can (targeted data collection, augmentation, a per-slice threshold); (4) for whatever remains, write it into the model card as a measured limitation with the number attached, and derive a use limitation from it.





15 · Dataset bias: domain and demographic skew math


Every sentiment dataset encodes the conditions under which it was collected. Those conditions become model behaviour.



Domain skew


A model trained on movie reviews learns that "predictable" is negative and "unpredictable" is positive. Deploy it on car reviews and it will confidently invert both. This is not an exotic edge case; it is the single most common cause of a production sentiment system underperforming its evaluation numbers. The mechanism is simple: polarity is a property of a word in a domain, and the training set only ever contained one domain.


The associated trap is a sampling artefact. Review corpora built from star ratings are typically constructed by taking the extremes — very high and very low ratings — and discarding the middle, because the middle is ambiguous to label. A model trained on that distribution has never seen a lukewarm review, and when it meets one it will not answer "neutral" (a class it may not even have); it will answer positive or negative, with high confidence, essentially at random. Check the rating histogram of your training data before you trust anything the model says about moderate opinions.



Temporal skew


Language about products moves. Product names, slang, the meaning of "sick" or "mid", the platform conventions for irony — all shift within a year or two. A model frozen at training time degrades continuously afterwards, and it degrades silently, because nothing in the serving path recomputes accuracy. Schedule re-evaluation on freshly labelled samples and track the metric over time; that time series is the only warning you will get.



Demographic skew


Two distinct mechanisms, and they need different responses.


    
  
  • Skew in who is represented. If the training text is overwhelmingly from one language variety, one register, or one platform, the model has less evidence about everyone else and will be less accurate for them. This shows up directly in a sliced evaluation.
    • 
  
  • Learned association with identity terms. Sentiment systems can attach polarity to words that merely mention a group, because in the training data those words co-occurred with charged discussion. The result is a model that scores otherwise-identical sentences differently depending on a name or a group term. Kiritchenko and Mohammad's "Examining Gender and Race Bias in Two Hundred Sentiment Analysis Systems" (arxiv.org/abs/1805.04508) built exactly this test — pairs of sentences identical except for a first name associated with a different gender or racial group — and found systematic score differences across a large number of deployed systems.
    • 



The second mechanism is directly testable and you should test for it. Build a small template set — "<NAME> made me feel angry", "<NAME> told me the news" — instantiate it across name lists, and measure the distribution of scores per group. Any difference is produced entirely by the substituted token, because everything else in the sentence is held constant. Report the size of the difference; it is one of the strongest pieces of evidence a bias audit can produce, and it takes an afternoon.



Document the dataset, not just the model


Both of the standard documentation formats exist for this reason and both are short enough to actually write: datasheets for datasets (Gebru et al., arxiv.org/abs/1803.09010) records how the data was collected, who is in it, and what it should not be used for; model cards (Mitchell et al., arxiv.org/abs/1810.03993) records intended use, evaluation data, sliced results, and limitations. A sentiment service without both is a service whose behaviour nobody can predict.

Human-review triggers


Limitations are enforceable only when they are wired into the serving path. Define the triggers explicitly, in code, and log every one:


    
  
  • Low confidence. After calibration, any prediction inside an abstention band (for example 0.40–0.60) is not acted on; it is queued.
    • 
  
  • Aspect conflict. Any document whose aspect-level labels disagree in sign is not summarized by a single document-level label — the review of the food-good/service-bad review in §8 is precisely this case.
    • 
  
  • Flagged slice. Any document that falls into a slice with a documented performance gap.
    • 
  
  • Consequence threshold. Any prediction that would trigger an irreversible action — closing an account, escalating a complaint, changing a person's record — regardless of confidence.
    • 




What the system is not measuring


Two boundaries are worth stating plainly in every model card, because they are routinely crossed by well-meaning teams.


First, sentiment is not intent and it is not character. The model estimates the polarity of a text under one annotation scheme. It does not observe motive, honesty, or state of mind, and no confidence value converts a polarity estimate into evidence about a person. A decision that treats "this message was scored negative" as "this person is hostile" has substituted a measurement for an inference the measurement cannot support.


Second, aggregate scores are not individual scores. A sentiment system with 85% accuracy can support a claim about a trend across ten thousand documents while supporting essentially nothing about any single one of them. Design the product so aggregation is the default output and per-document labels are shown with their confidence and their limitations — because the interface that shows a bare label on one document is the interface that invites the decision the limitations forbid.




  Bringing it back to the product. The MoodLens service in this topic is a fine-tuned transformer classifier plus an aspect-extraction step behind a FastAPI service, with predictions persisted for audit and a Streamlit surface that shows scoring, an aspect breakdown, model performance (confusion matrix, ROC, calibration), a bias audit, and a model card. Every one of those panels is a section of these notes: §10–§12 are the performance panel, §14 is the bias-audit panel, §16 is the model card. The audit log exists so that the sliced evaluation can be recomputed later on real traffic rather than on the split you happened to hold out.
