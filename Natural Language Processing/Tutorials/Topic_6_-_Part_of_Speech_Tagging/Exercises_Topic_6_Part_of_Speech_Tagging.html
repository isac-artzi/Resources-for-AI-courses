That is what a suffix rule is: a bet on a correlation, not a fact about the language. The young error is different in kind: nothing fired, the default took over, and the default is a prior about new vocabulary that happens not to hold for a common adjective. Coverage errors like this shrink automatically as the training corpus grows.

Exercise 4 — Split accuracy known / unknown warm-up

Using your results from Exercise 3, report three numbers: overall token accuracy, accuracy on tokens the table knew, and accuracy on tokens routed to the cascade. Also give the unknown-token rate. Then explain, in two sentences, why the overall number alone would be a misleading thing to put in a report.

Show solution

Known tokens (in the table): A, . — that is 2 tokens, both correct.
Unknown tokens (routed to the cascade): young, dog, barked, loudly, during, morning, training — 7 tokens, 3 correct.

overall accuracy = 5 / 9 = 55.6%
known-token accuracy = 2 / 2 = 100.0%
unknown-token accuracy = 3 / 7 = 42.9%
unknown-token rate = 7 / 9 = 77.8%

Why the overall number misleads. It is a weighted average of two completely different regimes, and the weight is set by the unknown-token rate — which is a property of your corpus size and domain match, not of your tagger. Two taggers with identical rules can report wildly different overall accuracies purely because one was evaluated on text closer to its training data, so without the split nobody can tell whether you improved the model or changed the test set.

On a real treebank the weighting flips: the unknown rate is a few percent, so overall accuracy is dominated by known tokens and lands near 90%. The unknown-token accuracy stays around this bad, which is exactly why almost all of the baseline's remaining error lives in unknown words.

Exercise 5 — The ambiguity ceiling medium

A training corpus gives these counts for the word that:

that -> {SCONJ: 120, DET: 70, PRON: 55}
  1. What tag does the lookup emit for that?
  2. What is the ceiling for any context-free tagger on this word?
  3. The test set contains 245 occurrences of that with the same tag proportions. How many of them does the lookup get wrong?
  4. The test set has 10,000 tokens in total. How many percentage points of overall accuracy does this one word type cost you?
  5. You are allowed to add exactly one feature to the tagger — the identity of the immediately preceding token. Sketch how that would help, and estimate whether it could plausibly close most of the gap.
Show solution

1. SCONJ, because 120 > 70 > 55. It emits SCONJ for every occurrence — that is what "context-free" means.

2. ceiling = 120 / (120 + 70 + 55) = 120 / 245 = 0.4898 = 49.0%. Slightly worse than a coin flip between two options, because there are three.

3. The lookup is right on the 120 SCONJ occurrences and wrong on the rest: 245 − 120 = 125 errors.

4. 125 / 10,000 = 0.0125 = 1.25 percentage points. One spelling, out of thousands of word types, costs more than a point of accuracy on its own. This is the concrete reason the last few points of POS tagging accuracy are so expensive: they are concentrated in a handful of high-frequency ambiguous function words.

5. Key the lookup on the pair (previous token, word) instead of the word alone, falling back to the word-only table when the pair is unseen. The previous token is highly diagnostic here:

  • preceded by a verb of speech or thought (said, knew, thought) → SCONJ
  • preceded by a verb like like, saw, bought and followed by a noun → DET
  • preceded by a noun → PRON (relative clause: the book that I read)

Could it close most of the gap? Plausibly a good part of it, since the three uses have quite different left contexts. But it trades one problem for another: the number of (previous token, word) pairs is enormous, so most pairs are unseen at test time and you fall back to the word-only answer constantly. That data-sparsity wall is precisely what a contextual encoder gets around — it does not need to have seen this preceding word, only words whose representations are similar.

Exercise 7 — Macro-F1 from a confusion matrix medium

A tagger is evaluated on a corpus with three tags. Rows are gold, columns are predicted.

pred NOUNpred VERBpred ADJ
gold NOUN80155
gold VERB10882
gold ADJ622

Compute, showing your work: total tokens, token accuracy, per-tag precision, recall and F1, and macro-F1. Round to three decimals. Then say in one sentence what the gap between accuracy and macro-F1 is telling you.

Show solution

Row sums (support): NOUN 80+15+5 = 100, VERB 10+88+2 = 100, ADJ 6+2+2 = 10. Total = 210 tokens.

Column sums (times predicted): NOUN 80+10+6 = 96, VERB 15+88+2 = 105, ADJ 5+2+2 = 9.

Accuracy = diagonal / total = (80 + 88 + 2) / 210 = 170 / 210 = 0.810.

tagprecision = M[t][t]/colrecall = M[t][t]/rowF1 = 2PR/(P+R)support
NOUN80/96 = 0.83380/100 = 0.8002(0.833)(0.800)/1.633 = 0.816100
VERB88/105 = 0.83888/100 = 0.8802(0.838)(0.880)/1.718 = 0.859100
ADJ2/9 = 0.2222/10 = 0.2002(0.222)(0.200)/0.422 = 0.21110

macro-F1 = (0.816 + 0.859 + 0.211) / 3 = 1.886 / 3 = 0.629

What the gap says. Accuracy 0.810 versus macro-F1 0.629: the model is competent on the two tags that carry 95% of the tokens and has essentially failed on ADJ, which has 10 tokens and therefore almost no influence on accuracy but a full one-third share of macro-F1.

Two details worth noticing. First, ADJ's precision (0.222) is even worse than it looks — the model predicts ADJ nine times and is right twice, so it over-predicts a tag it cannot recognise. Second, with a support of 10 this F1 is extremely unstable: one more correct ADJ token moves recall from 0.200 to 0.300 and macro-F1 by roughly three points. Always print the support column so a reader can discount rows like this.

Exercise 8 — 97.2% accuracy, 71.4% macro-F1 medium

You fine-tune a transformer on an English treebank with the 17 universal tags and get 97.2% token accuracy and 71.4% macro-F1. Your lookup baseline gets 91.0% accuracy and 78.5% macro-F1.

Explain what is most likely going on, what you would look at first to confirm it, and whether you should describe the transformer as better than the baseline. Note that 71.4% is suspiciously close to a round fraction of 17.

Show solution

What is going on. 71.4% ≈ 12/17 = 0.7059, and more tellingly 5/17 = 0.294, so a macro-F1 near 0.71 with 17 tags is consistent with roughly five tags scoring close to zero and the rest close to one. The transformer has almost certainly stopped predicting several rare tags entirely — candidates are X, SYM, INTJ, and possibly PART and SCONJ. A tag that is never predicted has precision 0 (under zero_division=0) and recall 0, so F1 = 0, and each such tag costs 1/17 ≈ 5.9 macro-F1 points while costing almost no accuracy because it has almost no tokens.

The baseline does not have this problem, and that is not luck. The lookup emits whatever tag it counted, so if the training corpus ever tagged a word SYM the table will happily emit SYM. It has no majority-class pressure. That is exactly why its macro-F1 (78.5%) beats the transformer's despite 6 points worse accuracy.

What to look at first.

  1. The per-tag table: precision, recall, F1 and support for all 17 tags, for both models. The story should be immediately visible as a set of rows with F1 = 0.000.
  2. The column sums of the transformer's confusion matrix. A column that sums to zero means the model never predicts that tag at all.
  3. The tag distribution of the training split. If a tag has fewer than a few dozen training tokens, cross-entropy on a heavily imbalanced set will rationally trade it away.
  4. Whether num_labels exceeds the number of tags actually present in your data. A label id that never occurs in training will score F1 = 0 forever and drag macro-F1 down for no reason at all — check this before you conclude anything about the model.

Is the transformer better? Say both numbers and let them stand. The defensible statement is: "the transformer reduces token-level error by (0.972 − 0.910) / (1 − 0.910) = 69% relative to the baseline, but its macro-F1 is 7 points worse because it has collapsed on N rare tags, which the baseline still predicts." Whether that trade is acceptable depends on the consumer — a parser cares about the frequent tags and will prefer the transformer; a pipeline that keys on SYM or X will not. Reporting only accuracy here would hide a real regression, and reporting only macro-F1 would hide a real improvement.

What to do. Class weighting or oversampling the rare tags, and re-checking num_labels. Then re-report both numbers.

Exercise 11 — Code: build_lookup hard

Write build_lookup(corpus). The input is a list of sentences, each a list of (word, tag) pairs. It must lowercase words, count (word, tag) pairs, and return a dict mapping each lowercased word to its most-frequent tag, breaking ties by tag name in ascending alphabetical order. Also return the raw counts so a caller can compute ceilings.

Your function must produce exactly this output on the Exercise 1 corpus:

lookup["bark"]  == "VERB"
counts["bark"]  == {"VERB": 2, "NOUN": 1}
lookup["the"]   == "DET"
len(lookup)     == 13
# and for the tie-breaking test:
build_lookup([[("saw","NOUN")],[("saw","VERB")]])[0]["saw"] == "NOUN"
Show solution 
from collections import defaultdict, Counter

def build_lookup(corpus):
    """corpus: list of sentences, each a list of (word, tag) pairs.
    Returns (lookup, counts).
      lookup: {lowercased word -> most frequent tag}
      counts: {lowercased word -> Counter({tag: n})}
    Ties are broken by tag name ascending, so the result is reproducible."""
    counts = defaultdict(Counter)
    for sentence in corpus:
        for word, tag in sentence:
            counts[word.lower()][tag] += 1

    lookup = {}
    for word, dist in counts.items():
        # sort by (-count, tag): highest count first, then alphabetical
        lookup[word] = sorted(dist.items(), key=lambda kv: (-kv[1], kv[0]))[0][0]
    return lookup, dict(counts)


def ceiling(counts, word):
    """Best possible accuracy of any context-free tagger on this word type."""
    dist = counts[word.lower()]
    return max(dist.values()) / sum(dist.values())

Verification on the Exercise 1 corpus:

corpus = [
    [("The","DET"),("dogs","NOUN"),("bark","VERB"),(".","PUNCT")],

print(lookup["the"])           # DET
print(len(lookup))             # 13
print(round(ceiling(counts, "bark"), 3))   # 0.667

# tie-breaking: NOUN and VERB both have count 1, NOUN sorts first
print(build_lookup([[("saw","NOUN")],[("saw","VERB")]])[0]["saw"])   # NOUN

Why the tie-break is not a detail. Counter.most_common() resolves ties by insertion order, which depends on the order your corpus reader happened to walk the files. Two runs over the same data with the files in a different order would then produce different lookup tables and different accuracy numbers — a reproducibility bug that is very hard to trace because nothing about it looks like a bug. Sorting on (-count, tag) costs nothing and removes the whole class of problem.

Why return the counts. The dict of most-frequent tags is all you need for inference, but the counts are what let you compute ceilings, split errors into ambiguity versus coverage, and report how much of your test set is ambiguous. Throwing them away means recomputing the whole table when you get to the error analysis.

Show solution 
def align_labels(word_ids, tags, label2id):
    """Assign each word's tag to the FIRST subword of that word.
    Continuation subwords and special tokens get -100, which
    torch.nn.CrossEntropyLoss ignores by default."""
    labels, prev = [], None
    for wid in word_ids:
        if wid is None:            # [CLS], [SEP], padding
            labels.append(-100)
        elif wid != prev:          # first subword of a new word
            labels.append(label2id[tags[wid]])
        else:                      # continuation subword
            labels.append(-100)
        prev = wid
    return labels


def read_back(word_ids, pred_ids, id2label):
    """Inverse of align_labels: one predicted tag per original word,
    taken from the first subword of that word."""
    out, prev = [], None
    for wid, pid in zip(word_ids, pred_ids):
        if wid is not None and wid != prev:
            out.append(id2label[pid])
        prev = wid
    return out

# ['DET', 'NOUN', 'NOUN', 'VERB', 'DET', 'NOUN', 'PUNCT']

Three things this code gets right that hand-rolled versions usually get wrong:

  • The prev variable, not a set. Tracking "words already seen" in a set works for a single sentence but breaks the moment you use overflowing/striding windows, where the same word id can legitimately reappear in a later window. Comparing with the previous position is the version that survives.
  • The assert. One non--100 label per word, always. This single line would have caught every bug in Exercise 10. Put it in your dataset-building function, not in a notebook cell you will delete.
  • The same word_ids drives both directions. Training and inference use one function each, both keyed on word_ids(), so the conventions cannot drift apart.

One thing to watch: if the tokenizer truncates, word_ids is shorter than the full sentence and read_back returns fewer tags than you have words. Compare lengths before scoring, or you will silently misalign your evaluation instead of your training.

Exercise 13 — Code: compute_metrics hard

Write compute_metrics(logits, labels, id2label) returning token accuracy, macro-F1, and the list of the five largest off-diagonal confusion cells as (gold, predicted, count). It must ignore every position whose label is -100. Do not use scikit-learn for the confusion matrix — build it yourself so you know what is in it.

Stated expected output for this tiny case (3 tags, 2 sequences of length 4):

id2label = {0: "NOUN", 1: "VERB", 2: "ADJ"}
labels = [[-100, 0, 1, -100],
          [-100, 0, 2, -100]]
preds  = [[   1, 0, 0,    1],       # positions with label -100 must be ignored
          [   2, 1, 2,    0]]

# tokens actually scored: (0,0) (1,0) (0,1) (2,2)
# accuracy = 2/4 = 0.5
# per-tag F1: NOUN P=1/2 R=1/2 F1=0.5 ; VERB P=0 R=0 F1=0 ; ADJ P=1/1 R=1/1 F1=1.0
# macro-F1 = (0.5 + 0.0 + 1.0)/3 = 0.5
# top confusions: [("NOUN","VERB",1), ("VERB","NOUN",1)]
Show solution 
import numpy as np

def compute_metrics(preds, labels, id2label):
    preds  = np.asarray(preds)
    labels = np.asarray(labels)

    mask   = labels != -100          # THE line that matters
    y_true = labels[mask]
    y_pred = preds[mask]

    n = len(id2label)
    M = np.zeros((n, n), dtype=int)
    for g, p in zip(y_true, y_pred):
        M[g, p] += 1

    total    = M.sum()
    accuracy = np.trace(M) / total if total else 0.0

    f1s = []
    for t in range(n):
        tp = M[t, t]
        col = M[:, t].sum()          # times tag t was predicted
        row = M[t, :].sum()          # times tag t was the gold answer
        prec = tp / col if col else 0.0
        rec  = tp / row if row else 0.0
        f1   = 2 * prec * rec / (prec + rec) if (prec + rec) else 0.0
        if row > 0:                  # only tags present in the gold data
            f1s.append(f1)
    macro_f1 = float(np.mean(f1s)) if f1s else 0.0

    cells = [(id2label[g], id2label[p], int(M[g, p]))
             for g in range(n) for p in range(n)
             if g != p and M[g, p] > 0]
    cells.sort(key=lambda c: (-c[2], c[0], c[1]))

    return {"accuracy": float(accuracy),
            "macro_f1": macro_f1,
            "top_confusions": cells[:5]}

Running the stated case:

id2label = {0: "NOUN", 1: "VERB", 2: "ADJ"}
labels = [[-100, 0, 1, -100], [-100, 0, 2, -100]]
preds  = [[   1, 0, 0,    1], [   2, 1, 2,    0]]
print(compute_metrics(preds, labels, id2label))
# {'accuracy': 0.5, 'macro_f1': 0.5,
#  'top_confusions': [('NOUN', 'VERB', 1), ('VERB', 'NOUN', 1)]}

Work it through by hand to confirm. The mask keeps four positions: gold NOUN/pred NOUN, gold VERB/pred NOUN, gold NOUN/pred VERB, gold ADJ/pred ADJ. So

          pred NOUN  pred VERB  pred ADJ
gold NOUN     1          1          0
gold VERB     1          0          0
gold ADJ      0          0          1

Accuracy = (1 + 0 + 1) / 4 = 0.5. NOUN: P = 1/2, R = 1/2, F1 = 0.5. VERB: P = 0/1 = 0, R = 0/1 = 0, F1 = 0. ADJ: P = 1/1, R = 1/1, F1 = 1.0. macro-F1 = 1.5/3 = 0.5.

Three decisions in that code that change the numbers:

  • mask = labels != -100. Omit it and you score the special tokens and continuation subwords too. Because -100 is not a valid class index, what happens next depends on your array library — best case you crash, worst case you index from the end and quietly score garbage.
  • if row > 0 when averaging. Only tags that actually occur in the gold data enter the macro average. Include a tag with zero support and you add a guaranteed 0.0 term for a tag nobody was ever asked to predict — the same trap as setting num_labels too high.
  • Zero-division returns 0, not skip. A tag the model never predicts gets precision 0, and therefore F1 0, and therefore drags macro-F1 down. That is the honest behaviour: failing to ever predict a tag is a failure, not a missing value. It matches sklearn.metrics.f1_score(..., average="macro", zero_division=0).

For the Trainer API, wrap it: compute_metrics receives an EvalPrediction whose first element is logits, so start with preds = np.argmax(logits, axis=-1) and pass that in.

Exercise 14 — Defend your baseline hard

A reviewer of your project report writes: "Comparing a transformer to a dictionary lookup is not a meaningful evaluation. The lookup is not a model. You should compare against something modern."

Write a response of roughly 250 words. It must (a) state what a baseline is for, (b) give the specific property of the lookup that a stronger baseline would not have, (c) show one concrete error analysis that only the lookup makes possible, and (d) concede the one thing the reviewer is right about.

Show solution

(a) What a baseline is for. A baseline exists to decompose a score. Any accuracy figure mixes two contributions: how easy the task is, and how good the model is. The most-frequent-tag lookup measures exactly the part of POS tagging that is solvable from word identity alone. Whatever the transformer scores above it is, by construction, the contribution of context — which is the only thing this project claims the transformer is for. A "more modern" baseline would blur that decomposition rather than sharpen it, because it too would use context, and the difference between the two models would no longer isolate anything.

(b) The property a stronger baseline lacks. The lookup is completely inspectable: the model is a table of counts, so every prediction reduces to one printable line, and the model has no random seed, no initialisation, and no training procedure that could have gone wrong. When it is wrong, the cause is visible rather than inferred. No trained model has that property, which is why swapping it for something stronger costs more than it buys.

(c) The error analysis it makes possible. Take the sentence "The bark was quiet ." The lookup predicts VERB for bark. Its entry is {VERB: 2, NOUN: 1}, so the ceiling for any context-free tagger on this word is 2/3 = 66.7%, and this token is in the losing third. That single line establishes three things at once: the error is caused by lexical ambiguity, it is not fixable by more training data of the same kind, and the determiner immediately to the left is the evidence a contextual model must use. Because the baseline partitions all of its errors into ambiguity, rule, and coverage, I can state what fraction of the remaining error a contextual model could even in principle remove — before training one.

(d) Where the reviewer is right. The lookup is not a competitive tagger and should never be presented as one; beating it is necessary, not sufficient. If the report's only claim were "we beat the lookup", that would be a weak claim. The lookup is a floor and a diagnostic instrument, not a rival. A published state-of-the-art number for the same treebank and tag set belongs in the report as well — as a ceiling — so the reader can see both how much of the task the transformer captured and how much room is left.

Marking note. A good answer names the decomposition argument (a) explicitly, and gives a concrete worked error (c) with actual counts rather than a description of one. An answer that omits the concession (d) has argued for the wrong thing: the claim is not that the lookup is a good tagger, it is that it is a good instrument.
If you finished all fourteen. Do the same three-way error partition (ambiguity / rule / coverage) on your own baseline over a real English treebank from universaldependencies.org, then predict — before training — how much your transformer can improve. Write the prediction down. Comparing it with what actually happens is the most informative single thing you can do in this topic.
