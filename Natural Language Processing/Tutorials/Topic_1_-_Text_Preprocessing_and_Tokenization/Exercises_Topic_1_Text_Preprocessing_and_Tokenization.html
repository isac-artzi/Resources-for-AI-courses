Exercise 1 — Count the tokens

Take this string exactly as written:

Dr. Smith's e-mail (dr.smith@example.com) arrived at 9:45 a.m. — it wasn't urgent.
  1. How many tokens does a pure whitespace split produce? List them.
  2. How many tokens does the regular expression [A-Za-z0-9]+|[^\sA-Za-z0-9] produce — that is, "a run of letters or digits, or a single non-space character that is neither"? List them.
  3. Name four things in this string that the second tokenizer damaged.
Show solution

1. Whitespace split — 12 tokens.

Dr.   Smith's   e-mail   (dr.smith@example.com)   arrived   at
9:45   a.m.   —   it   wasn't   urgent.

Nothing is broken, but nearly every token carries attached punctuation, so urgent. and urgent are different types.

2. Regex split — 33 tokens.

Dr  .  Smith  '  s  e  -  mail  (  dr  .  smith  @  example  .  com  )
arrived  at  9  :  45  a  .  m  .  —  it  wasn  '  t  urgent  .

Count them in groups of ten if you lost your place: Dr . Smith ' s e - mail ( dr (10), . smith @ example . com ) arrived at 9 (20), : 45 a . m . — it wasn ' (30), t urgent . (33).

3. Four things it damaged.

  • The email address. One meaningful unit became eight tokens, none of which is useful, and all of which are rare types.
  • The contraction. wasn't became wasn + ' + t. The negation is now unrecoverable by any downstream rule that looks for a negation word.
  • The abbreviation a.m. became a . m . — and the final period is now indistinguishable from a sentence-ending period, so sentence splitting downstream will cut the sentence in the wrong place.
  • The hyphenated word e-mail became e + - + mail, so it no longer matches email or e-mail as a unit.

Also acceptable: 9:45 lost its structure, and Dr. lost the marker that distinguishes an abbreviation from a sentence end.

Exercise 2 — Which normalization makes these equal?

For each pair, say which of the four operations makes the two strings compare equal: (a) ASCII lowercasing, (b) Unicode NFC, (c) Unicode NFKC, (d) NFD followed by deleting combining marks (accent stripping). More than one may apply, and for one pair none of the four is enough.

#string Astring B
1Cafécafe
2ﬁle (with the fi ligature)file
3e + U+0301 combining acuteé (U+00E9)
4STRASSEstraße
5Q2q2
Show solution
  1. Café / cafe — needs (a) and (d). Lowercasing alone leaves café against cafe. Accent stripping alone leaves Cafe against cafe. Both together give cafe = cafe.
  2. ﬁle / file — needs (c) NFKC. The ligature is a compatibility variant, so only the K forms map it to f + i. NFC leaves it alone, because the ligature is not canonically equivalent to the two letters.
  3. Both forms of é — (b) NFC works, and so does plain NFD, and so does (c). This is canonical equivalence: NFC composes both to U+00E9, NFD decomposes both to e + U+0301. Either direction works as long as you apply the same form to both sides. Note that (d) also makes them equal, but by deleting the accent, which is a different and more destructive claim.
  4. STRASSE / straße — none of the four. ASCII lowercasing gives strasse against straße; no normalization form maps ß to ss, because they are not equivalent characters. You need full Unicode case folding, which does map ß to ss — in Python that is str.casefold(), not str.lower(). This is the single most common reason a "lowercase everything" pipeline silently fails on German text.
  5. Q2 / q2 — (a) alone. Pure ASCII case difference.

The transferable point. "Normalize the text" is not one operation. Write down which form you apply and where, because the pipeline that indexes your documents and the pipeline that handles a query at serving time must apply the same one, or the match fails for reasons no log will show you.

Exercise 3 — The stop list ate the meaning

Stop list:

{ a, an, and, be, but, do, for, i, in, is, it, no, not, of, or,
  the, to, was, were, would, you }

Sentence: I would not say the film was bad.

  1. Lowercase, drop punctuation, remove stop words. Write the surviving token list.
  2. A bag-of-words sentiment classifier is trained on token lists produced by exactly this pipeline. What label will it predict for this document, and what did the writer actually mean?
  3. Propose two different fixes. For each, state one cost.
Show solution

1. Survivors: say, film, bad. Everything else — i, would, not, the, was — is on the list, and the period is dropped as punctuation.

2. The classifier sees a document whose only polarity-bearing token is bad, and will predict negative. The writer used litotes — "I would not say the film was bad" is a guarded positive, or at worst a neutral. The preprocessing deleted the single token, not, on which the entire reading depended, and it did so before the model ever saw the text. No amount of model capacity recovers this; the information is gone.

3. Two fixes, with costs.

  • Fix A — remove negations from the stop list. Keep not, no, never, nor, and the n't suffix. Cheap and immediate. Cost: a bag of words has no order, so not now floats free — the model learns only that documents containing not lean negative, which is itself wrong roughly as often as it is right ("not bad").
  • Fix B — negation-scope marking before removal. Rewrite tokens from a negator to the next punctuation mark with a prefix: say film NOT_bad. Now NOT_bad is its own feature and the ordering problem disappears. Cost: vocabulary roughly doubles for the affected words, each new feature has less training data, and the scope rule ("to the next punctuation") is wrong for plenty of sentences.
  • Fix C — stop removing stop words and use a model with order. Any sequence model, and certainly a transformer, uses function words as signal. Cost: compute, and a heavier serving footprint for what may be a task a linear model could do.

Two of the three are acceptable answers; the graded part is naming a real cost rather than presenting a fix as free.

  2. The word saw has two different lemmas. Give both, and say what a lemmatizer needs to know to choose.

1.

The cost — caring and car colliding — is tolerable in a search index where recall matters more than precision and where the ranker has other signals; it would not be tolerable if the conflated form were shown to a user or used as a linguistic claim.

Exercise 6 — Compute an OOV rate

A corpus has exactly these types and counts:

the         500
of          300
model       120
token        60
word         40
subword      20
tokenizer    10
lemmatize     6
(44 further types, each occurring exactly once)
  1. How many tokens and how many types does the corpus contain?
  2. You build a word-level vocabulary from the five most frequent types. Compute the OOV token rate and the OOV type rate.
  3. Now use the eight most frequent types. Compute both rates again.
  4. The vocabulary grew by 60% between (2) and (3). Explain, in terms of the shape of the frequency distribution, why the token rate improved so much more than the type rate.
  5. What vocabulary size drives the OOV token rate to exactly zero, and why is that number useless in practice?

Work all five out before using the widget.

Widget · OOV calculator over exactly this table

Show solution

1. Tokens: 500 + 300 + 120 + 60 + 40 + 20 + 10 + 6 + 44 = 1,100. Types: 8 named + 44 singletons = 52.

2. V = 5 (the, of, model, token, word). Covered tokens = 500 + 300 + 120 + 60 + 40 = 1,020. Unknown tokens = 1,100 − 1,020 = 80.

OOVtoken = 80 / 1100 = 0.0727 = 7.27%
OOVtype = (52 − 5) / 52 = 47 / 52 = 90.4%

3. V = 8 (adds subword, tokenizer, lemmatize). Covered tokens = 1,020 + 20 + 10 + 6 = 1,056. Unknown tokens = 44 — one for each singleton.

OOVtoken = 44 / 1100 = 0.04 = 4.00%
OOVtype = (52 − 8) / 52 = 44 / 52 = 84.6%

4. The distribution is extremely top-heavy: the two most frequent types alone account for 800 of the 1,100 tokens, and the 44 singletons account for 4% of the tokens while making up 85% of the distinct word list. Adding three types to the vocabulary added 36 tokens of coverage — a large share of what was left uncovered — while removing only three of the 47 unknown types. The token rate is a frequency-weighted measure, so it is dominated by the head of the distribution; the type rate is unweighted, so it is dominated by the tail. This is Zipf's law doing exactly what it always does, and it is why the two rates must never be reported as if they were the same number.

5. V = 52, the full type count — nothing less can cover the singletons, because each contributes its own type. It is useless in practice for two reasons. First, by Heaps' law the type count keeps growing with corpus size, so "all types in the training corpus" is not "all types you will meet"; the first unseen document reintroduces unknowns. Second, a vocabulary that includes every singleton spends an embedding row on words the model saw once, which cannot produce a usable vector. The correct response is not a bigger word vocabulary but a different unit — subwords, whose pieces are all frequent by construction.

Exercise 8 — Encode an unseen word

Take the four merges you learned in Exercise 7, in order:

1. (a, n)    → an
2. (b, an)   → ban
3. (d, _)    → d_
4. (ban, an) → banan
  1. Encode bananas. Show the state after each of the four rules.
  2. Encode bans.
  3. The vocabulary contains an, ban and banan. A greedy longest-match tokenizer looking at bananas would take banan, then a, then s. BPE, applying its merges in order, produces the same answer here. Construct a merge list and a word for which the two procedures disagree, and say which one BPE follows.

The widget in Exercise 7 has an encode box — set it to run all four merges first.

Show solution

1. bananas — start from characters plus the end-of-word marker: b a n a n a s _

rule 1  (a,n) → an     :  b an an a s _      fired twice
rule 2  (b,an) → ban   :  ban an a s _       fired once
rule 3  (d,_) → d_     :  ban an a s _       no match
rule 4  (ban,an) → banan: banan a s _        fired once

result: banan | a | s | _        (4 pieces)

2. bans — start from b a n s _

rule 1  (a,n) → an     :  b an s _
rule 2  (b,an) → ban   :  ban s _
rule 3  (d,_) → d_     :  no match
rule 4  (ban,an) → banan: no match

result: ban | s | _              (3 pieces)

Neither word appeared in the training corpus, and both encoded without any unknown symbol. That is the property being bought.

3. A case where the two disagree. Suppose the learned merges are, in this order:

1. (a, b) → ab
2. (c, d) → cd
3. (ab, c) → abc

and the word is abcd (ignore the end-of-word marker for clarity).

  • BPE, merges in learned order: rule 1 gives ab c d; rule 2 matches c d and gives ab cd; rule 3 looks for ab followed by c and finds none, because the c was consumed by rule 2. Result: ab | cd — 2 pieces.
  • Greedy longest match over the vocabulary {a, b, c, d, ab, cd, abc}: at position 0 the longest vocabulary entry that matches is abc, leaving d. Result: abc | d — also 2 pieces, but a different segmentation, and therefore different token ids and a different input to the model.

BPE follows the first: merge order, not length. A merge learned earlier is applied earlier even when a longer piece exists in the vocabulary, and once a symbol has been consumed by an earlier merge it is not available to a later one. WordPiece is the algorithm that does the second thing — greedy longest match — which is exactly why the two produce different segmentations from vocabularies that look similar.

Exercise 9 — WordPiece by hand

Vocabulary:

un, token, ##ize, ##ization, ##er, ##ers, ##s, ##happy, ##ness
plus every single lowercase letter, both bare and with the ## prefix

WordPiece encoding is greedy longest-match-first, left to right, with ## on every piece after the first. Segment:

  1. tokenization
  2. tokenizers
  3. unhappiness
  4. untokenized

Then answer: item 4 fragments badly even though token is in the vocabulary. Why?

Show solution

1. tokenization → token + ##ization (2 pieces). At position 0 the longest vocabulary entry matching a prefix is token. From position 5 the remainder is ization, and ##ization is in the vocabulary. Clean.

2. tokenizers → token + ##ize + ##r + ##s (4 pieces). After token the remainder is izers. Longest match: ##izers is not in the vocabulary, ##izer is not either, ##ize is — so it is taken. The remainder is now rs. ##rs is not in the vocabulary, so ##r is taken, then ##s.

Notice what the greedy rule cost you: ##ers is in the vocabulary, and token + ##iz + ##ers would have been three pieces — but ##iz is not in the vocabulary, and in any case greedy longest-match never reconsiders an earlier choice. Greedy is fast and deterministic; it is not optimal.

3. unhappiness → un + ##h + ##a + ##p + ##p + ##i + ##ness (7 pieces). At position 0, un is the longest match. The remainder is happiness. Now the trap: the vocabulary has ##happy, not ##happi, and happiness begins h-a-p-p-i. The match fails at every length down to the single letter ##h. The same happens for ##a, ##p, ##p, ##i — and only then does the remainder become ness, which matches ##ness.

One character of spelling difference between happy and happi turned a two-piece word into a seven-piece word. This is what a domain shift does to fertility.

4. untokenized → un + ##t + ##o + ##k + ##e + ##n + ##i + ##z + ##e + ##d (10 pieces).

Why: token is in the vocabulary only in its word-initial form. There is no ##token. Once un has been emitted, every subsequent piece must carry the ## prefix, and ##token does not exist, so the matcher falls all the way to single letters. The ## marker is not cosmetic — it partitions the vocabulary into two disjoint sets, word-initial and word-internal, and a piece present in one is absent from the other unless it was added twice.

This is a real property of real vocabularies: a subword tokenizer's behaviour on X tells you very little about its behaviour on preX, and it is why fertility must be measured on your own text rather than assumed.

Exercise 10 — Write fertility()

Write a function with this signature:

def fertility(text: str, pieces_fn) -> tuple[int, int, float]:
    """Return (n_words, n_pieces, pieces_per_word).

    n_words   : whitespace-delimited words in `text`
    n_pieces  : total pieces produced by applying `pieces_fn` to each word
    pieces_per_word : n_pieces / n_words, rounded to 2 decimal places
                      (return 0.0 when the text has no words)
    """

It must satisfy exactly this:

FAKE = {
    "the":          ["the"],
    "tokenizer":    ["token", "##izer"],
    "preprocesses": ["pre", "##process", "##es"],
    "text":         ["text"],
}

print(fertility("the tokenizer preprocesses text", lambda w: FAKE[w]))
# expected:  (4, 7, 1.75)

print(fertility("   ", lambda w: FAKE[w]))
# expected:  (0, 0, 0.0)
Show solution 
def fertility(text, pieces_fn):
    words = text.split()          # split() with no argument handles
                                  # runs of spaces, tabs and newlines,
                                  # and returns [] for whitespace-only input
    if not words:
        return (0, 0, 0.0)

    n_pieces = sum(len(pieces_fn(w)) for w in words)
    return (len(words), n_pieces, round(n_pieces / len(words), 2))

Checking the first case by hand: four words; pieces 1 + 2 + 3 + 1 = 7; 7 / 4 = 1.75, which needs no rounding. Output (4, 7, 1.75).

Three things the tests are actually checking.

  • text.split() with no argument, not text.split(" "). The latter returns ['', '', '', ''] for a string of spaces, giving n_words = 4 and a division that produces the wrong answer instead of an error you would notice.
  • The empty guard returns 0.0, a float, matching the declared return type. Returning 0 would compare equal in Python but is a different type in a schema that a service will serialize.
  • Fertility is pieces per word, not pieces per character and not pieces per input token. If you divide by len(text) the number is meaningless across texts of different length.

Using it for real. With a Hugging Face fast tokenizer the callable is lambda w: tok.tokenize(w). Run it over a sample of your own traffic and over a sample of general text, and report both — the gap between them is the domain-shift cost, and it is the single most useful number in a tokenizer comparison.

Exercise 11 — Write oov_rates()

Write a function that computes both out-of-vocabulary rates from a frequency table:

def oov_rates(counts: dict[str, int], vocab: set[str]) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Return (token_rate, type_rate), each rounded to 4 decimal places.

    token_rate : share of all token occurrences whose type is not in `vocab`
    type_rate  : share of distinct types that are not in `vocab`
    Both are 0.0 when `counts` is empty.
    """

It must satisfy exactly this:

counts = {"the": 5, "cat": 3, "sat": 2, "mat": 1, "hat": 1}

print(oov_rates(counts, {"the", "cat"}))
# expected:  (0.3333, 0.6)

print(oov_rates(counts, set(counts)))
# expected:  (0.0, 0.0)

print(oov_rates({}, {"the"}))
# expected:  (0.0, 0.0)

Then answer in one sentence: why does the function take a frequency table rather than the raw text?

Show solution 
def oov_rates(counts, vocab):
    total_tokens = sum(counts.values())
    total_types  = len(counts)
    if total_types == 0 or total_tokens == 0:
        return (0.0, 0.0)

    oov_tokens = sum(n for t, n in counts.items() if t not in vocab)
    oov_types  = sum(1 for t in counts if t not in vocab)

    return (round(oov_tokens / total_tokens, 4),
            round(oov_types  / total_types,  4))

Checking the first case by hand. Total tokens = 5 + 3 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 12. Out-of-vocabulary types are sat, mat, hat, contributing 2 + 1 + 1 = 4 tokens.

token_rate = 4 / 12 = 0.33333… → 0.3333
type_rate = 3 / 5 = 0.6

Note that round(0.6, 4) is 0.6, not 0.6000 — Python prints the shortest representation, so the expected output is (0.3333, 0.6). If your test compares strings rather than floats this will look like a failure when it is not.

The two rates are genuinely different here: 33% of running text is unknown but 60% of the word list is. Reporting only one of them, without saying which, makes the result impossible to interpret.

Why a frequency table and not raw text. Because the function then has one job, has no opinion about tokenization, and is testable: the moment it accepts raw text it also owns the segmentation policy, and its result silently depends on a regular expression buried inside it. Counting is the caller's job; the caller already had to tokenize to build the table, and it should use the same tokenizer that the rest of the pipeline uses.

Exercise 12 — The 0.0% OOV report

A colleague's report contains this paragraph:

"We evaluated two tokenizers on 50,000 support tickets. The GPT-2 tokenizer achieved an out-of-vocabulary rate of 0.00%, while the BERT tokenizer achieved 0.31%. We therefore recommend the GPT-2 tokenizer."
  1. What is wrong with the comparison as a piece of reasoning?
  2. The 0.31% is not a mistake — explain mechanically where it comes from.
  3. Name three numbers you would report instead, and for each say what decision it informs.
Show solution

1. The metric cannot distinguish the two systems, because one of them cannot fail it. The GPT-2 tokenizer is byte-level: its base vocabulary is the 256 byte values, every string has a UTF-8 encoding, and therefore every possible input encodes without an unknown symbol. Its 0.00% is not a measurement of the support tickets — it is a restatement of the algorithm, and it would be 0.00% on random binary data. Comparing a number that is structurally fixed against one that varies is not a comparison, and the conclusion drawn from it is unsupported no matter which tokenizer is in fact better.

2. Where the 0.31% comes from. BERT's WordPiece has a real [UNK] token, and it emits one for a whole word whenever greedy longest-match fails at some position — which happens when the word contains a character outside the vocabulary's character set. On support tickets that means emoji, non-Latin scripts, unusual Unicode punctuation such as typographic quotation marks or non-breaking spaces, and control characters pasted from other systems. Note the granularity: a single unmapped character loses the entire word, not only that character.

3. Three numbers, and the decision each one informs.

  • Fertility (pieces per word) on these 50,000 tickets, per tokenizer. Decides cost and effective context length: fewer pieces means shorter sequences, cheaper attention, and more of the ticket fitting in a fixed window. Report the distribution, not only the mean, because a long tail matters more than the average.
  • Total piece count for the whole sample, per tokenizer. Converts directly to serving cost and latency, and it is the number a non-specialist reader can act on. A 15% difference in total pieces is a 15% difference in the bill.
  • Round-trip fidelity: the share of tickets for which decode(encode(t)) == t exactly. Decides whether the tokenizer is safe to put in front of anything that stores or displays the original text. This is where BERT's uncased normalization and its [UNK] both show up as real, quantified data loss, and it is the honest form of the comparison the colleague was reaching for.

Also creditable: the share of words that emitted more than one piece; per-language token cost if tickets are multilingual; and, if a downstream model already exists, the task metric itself — which outranks all tokenizer-internal measures when it is available.

The general lesson. Before reporting a metric, ask what value it takes when the system is trivially good and when it is trivially bad. A metric that is pinned at one end by construction tells you nothing.

Exercise 13 — Pick a tokenizer for two products

Two products, one week of work each:

  • Product A — code search. Users type a natural-language query; the system retrieves matching functions from a large Python and JavaScript repository.
  • Product B — a multilingual support inbox. Messages arrive in roughly twenty languages, including Japanese, Arabic and Finnish, mixed with product codes and the occasional emoji.

For each: choose a tokenization strategy, name one measurement you would run in the first day to justify the choice, and name one drawback you are accepting. Then say what changes if the two products have to share a single tokenizer.

Show solution

There is no single right answer here; a strong answer is specific about the trade and honest about the cost. One defensible pair:

Product A — code search. Use a subword tokenizer trained on code, not on prose — or, if training is out of scope for a week, a byte-level BPE from a model that was pre-trained on code. Reasons: identifiers such as user_id and getElementById are compositional and a code-trained vocabulary keeps their parts intact; indentation is significant in Python and code tokenizers commonly include whitespace-run pieces so that sixteen spaces cost one token rather than sixteen; and byte-level fallback means no [UNK] on the odd Unicode identifier or string literal.

  • Day-one measurement: fertility on a sample of a few thousand real functions, computed separately for identifiers and for whole files, comparing a prose tokenizer against a code tokenizer. Also count the tokens spent on leading whitespace — if it is a large share, the vocabulary is wrong for the corpus.
  • Drawback accepted: a code-trained vocabulary fragments ordinary English, and half of this product's input is English queries. You are optimizing for the document side at some cost to the query side.

Product B — multilingual inbox. Use a SentencePiece-style tokenizer from an established multilingual checkpoint, with byte fallback enabled. Reasons: Japanese has no whitespace, so any design that pre-tokenizes on spaces fails outright; SentencePiece treats the raw string including spaces, so the same pipeline works for every language without per-language rules; and reversibility matters because support text is stored, displayed back to agents, and quoted in replies.

  • Day-one measurement: tokens per message, broken down by language, on real traffic. If Japanese or Arabic messages cost three or four times more tokens than English for the same content, that is a fairness and cost problem you need to see before launch, not after — it means those users get less context and cost more to serve.
  • Drawback accepted: a vocabulary spread across twenty languages gives every individual language a smaller share than a monolingual vocabulary would, so fertility on English is worse than an English-only tokenizer would deliver.

If they must share one tokenizer. The requirements genuinely conflict: code wants whitespace pieces and identifier-friendly merges, multilingual text wants broad script coverage and no whitespace assumptions. Three honest options, in order of preference:

  1. Do not share. Two tokenizers are two config entries; the coupling buys almost nothing unless the two products also share a model. Establish first whether they actually do.
  2. Share a byte-level BPE trained on a mixture of both corpora. Byte level guarantees coverage of every script and every code point, so nothing breaks. Measure fertility on both corpora before and after and state the regression explicitly — expect both to get worse than their specialist alternatives, and decide whether the loss is acceptable.
  3. Share the multilingual tokenizer and accept the code fertility. Reasonable if code search is the smaller product; it means longer sequences and lower recall on long functions, which you should quantify rather than assume.

What makes an answer weak is naming a tokenizer without naming the measurement, or claiming a choice with no drawback. Every tokenizer choice trades sequence length against vocabulary size against coverage, and a design that appears to escape the trade has usually hidden it somewhere else.
