Topic 5 — Named Entity Recognition as Token Classification

Boundaries, BIO tags, character offsets, a CRF baseline, a fine-tuned transformer, and the metrics that tell you the truth about all of them.

What you should walk away with. (1) A precise definition of the task: a label per token, decoded into typed character spans. (2) Fluency with BIO and BIOES — including which sequences are illegal and how a model produces them anyway. (3) The ability to decode a tag sequence into character offsets by hand, and the knowledge that you should never compute those offsets by counting characters yourself. (4) A working mental model of a CRF: engineered features for the unary scores, a transition matrix for the sequence structure, Viterbi for decoding. (5) The subword alignment rule for transformer token classification, and what breaks when you get it wrong. (6) Entity-level precision, recall, and F1 computed by hand, and the ability to explain why token-level numbers are systematically kinder. (7) A vocabulary for errors — boundary, type confusion, unseen entity — and a threshold-driven review queue that turns model uncertainty into human work.

1 · What counts as an entity, and why boundaries are the hard part

A named entity is a span of text that refers to a specific thing by name. The standard newswire label set — the one used by the CoNLL-2003 shared task — has four types: PER (people), ORG (organisations), LOC (locations), and MISC (nationalities, events, products). Applied label sets add whatever the product needs: DATE, MONEY, DRUG, CASE_NUMBER, ACCOUNT_ID.

Two decisions define the task, and they are unequal in difficulty.

  • Type. Given the span Nordwind Air, is it an ORG or a LOC? Models get this right most of the time, because the surrounding words carry strong evidence: said, hired, and announced point at organisations; flew to and based in point at places.
  • Boundary. Where does the span start and where does it stop? This is where systems actually lose points, and it is where annotation guidelines spend most of their pages.

Consider four spans that all contain the word Bank:

TextGold spanWhy it is awkward
the European Central BankORG European Central BankThe determiner the is outside the entity by convention — but the convention is a choice, not a fact.
the Bank of England buildingORG Bank of EnglandThe entity contains a function word, of, and stops before a modified head noun.
a Nordwind Air flightORG Nordwind AirThe entity is used attributively; flight is not part of the name.
Cape Town International AirportLOC Cape Town International AirportA location name that contains another location name. Which one is gold depends entirely on the guideline.

Notice the pattern. The type is nearly always obvious to a human reader. The boundary is a decision that depends on a written rule, and if the annotators followed a different rule than the one you assume, your model will be graded against boundaries it never had a chance to learn.

The practical consequence. When you report a poor F1, your first diagnostic is not "the model doesn't understand organisations". It is "how many of my errors are the right type with the wrong edges?" Section 13 gives you the breakdown, and Section 12 gives you the metric that makes those errors visible in the first place.

Three further complications you should be able to name:

  • Nested entities. the University of Cape Town is an ORG containing the LOC Cape Town. Flat BIO tagging cannot represent both; you must pick the outer or the inner span. Nested NER needs a different formulation (span enumeration, or a stacked tagger).
  • Discontinuous entities. In clinical text, left and right ventricle encodes two findings sharing one head word. Standard sequence labelling cannot represent a span with a hole in it.
  • Overlapping types. Zurich in Zurich announced a fare increase is a place name used to refer to an organisation (metonymy). Guidelines differ on whether that is LOC or ORG; both answers are defensible and only one is in your gold file.

2 · NER as token classification math

The reduction that makes NER tractable is this: instead of predicting a set of spans directly — a search over O(n²) candidate spans with types — predict one label per token and reconstruct the spans afterwards.

input: x = (x₁, x₂, …, xn) tokens
output: y = (y₁, y₂, …, yn) yi ∈ L, the tag set
spans = decode(y)

With four entity types and the BIO scheme, |L| = 2 × 4 + 1 = 9. The model has to make n nine-way decisions rather than search a quadratic span space. That is the whole trick, and it costs you something real: a flat tag sequence cannot express nesting or overlap, as Section 1 noted.

Two families of model fill in the same skeleton:

CRF (linear-chain)Transformer token classifier
Per-token scoreWeighted sum of hand-written features: word identity, suffixes, capitalisation, POS, neighboursLinear head on top of a contextual hidden state hi ∈ ℝ768
Sequence structureExplicit learned transition matrix A[yi-1, yi]Usually none — each position is softmaxed independently
DecodingViterbi over the whole sentencePer-token argmax (optionally Viterbi, if a CRF layer is bolted on)
ContextA hand-chosen window, typically ±2 tokensThe entire sentence, through self-attention
What you inspect when it failsFeature weights — readable, one number per (feature, tag) pairAttention maps and probes — suggestive, not conclusive
Training data appetiteWorks usably on a few thousand sentencesBenefits from more, but arrives already knowing the language

The CRF is not in this course as history. It is in this course because it makes the structure of the task visible: the transition matrix is a table you can print, and its illegal-transition entries are large negative numbers you can point at. A transformer with a plain softmax head has no such table, which is precisely why it emits illegal tag sequences (Section 5).

3 · The BIO tagging scheme interactive

BIO (also written IOB2) encodes spans in three prefixes:

  • B-TYPE — this token begins an entity of that type.
  • I-TYPE — this token is inside an entity of that type, continuing the one that began earlier.
  • O — this token is outside any entity.

The B prefix exists for exactly one reason: to separate two entities of the same type that touch. Without it, Cape Town Frankfurt as two adjacent LOC entities is indistinguishable from one four-word LOC.

tokens:  The   European  Central  Bank   hired  Ana    Moreau  in   Frankfurt  .
BIO:     O     B-ORG     I-ORG    I-ORG  O      B-PER  I-PER   O    B-LOC      O
spans:         [------ ORG -------------]       [--- PER ----]      [-- LOC --]

Read the tags left to right and the spans fall out mechanically: a B- opens a span, matching I- tags extend it, and anything else closes it.

Demo 1 · BIO tagger playground

Assign a tag to every token. The widget validates the sequence, flags every illegal transition with the reason, decodes the spans you have actually produced, and scores you against the gold annotation at the entity level.

Two properties of the scheme that are worth stating explicitly, because they are the source of most confusion:

  • The tag set is flat. B-ORG and I-ORG are two unrelated integers as far as the cross-entropy loss is concerned. Nothing in a plain softmax head knows that I-ORG is "the continuation of" B-ORG. That relationship exists only in the decoder you write and in the transition matrix of a CRF.
  • The O class dominates. In newswire, roughly five in six tokens are O. A model that predicts O everywhere achieves high token accuracy and zero recall. This is why token accuracy is a useless headline number for NER and why Section 12 exists.

4 · BIOES and what the extra tags buy interactive

BIOES (also called BILOU) splits the same information five ways:

  • B-TYPE — first token of a multi-token entity.
  • I-TYPE — a middle token of a multi-token entity.
  • E-TYPE — last token of a multi-token entity.
  • S-TYPE — a single-token entity, all by itself.
  • O — outside.
tokens:  The   European  Central  Bank   hired  Ana    Moreau  in   Frankfurt  .
BIO:     O     B-ORG     I-ORG    I-ORG  O      B-PER  I-PER   O    B-LOC      O
BIOES:   O     B-ORG     I-ORG    E-ORG  O      B-PER  E-PER   O    S-LOC      O

The conversion is a pure function of the BIO sequence — no new information enters. What changes is the learning problem. Under BIOES the model must commit to an explicit end of every span, so the last token of an entity gets a dedicated class with its own evidence (a corporate suffix such as Ltd, a surname shape, a following verb). Under BIO, the end of a span is only implied by whatever comes next.

The trade-off: the tag set grows from 2T + 1 to 4T + 1, so each class has fewer training examples. On small datasets BIO usually wins; on larger ones BIOES often adds a small amount of F1 by sharpening boundaries. Treat it as a hyperparameter to try, not a doctrine, and never compare a BIO run against a BIOES run without converting both to spans first — the tag strings are not comparable, only the decoded entities are.

Demo 2 · BIO ⇄ BIOES converter

Edit the BIO sequence (space-separated) and watch the BIOES conversion, the round-trip back to BIO, and the decoded spans. The round-trip is lossless — proving that BIOES adds structure to the learning problem, not to the label.


  






5 · Invalid tag sequences: how they arise, what to do about them


A BIO sequence is well-formed if every I-TYPE is preceded by B-TYPE or I-TYPE of the same type. Everything else is illegal:



  
Illegal patternExampleWhat it would mean

  
O → I-XO I-PERAn entity continues that never began.

  
sentence start → I-XI-ORG …Same, at position 0.

  
B-X → I-Y, X ≠ YB-ORG I-LOCAn entity changes type halfway through.

  
I-X → I-Y, X ≠ YI-PER I-ORGSame, in the middle of a span.




How do these arise? A transformer token classifier applies a softmax at each position independently. Position i does not know what was argmaxed at position i−1; the two decisions share a hidden representation but not a decision. When the model is unsure between "this is a person" and "this is not an entity", it can easily emit O at the first token and I-PER at the second. Nothing in the loss forbids it, because the training labels were always well-formed and the model was never punished for producing something outside that space at inference.


Empirically, illegal transitions are rare in a well-trained model — low single-digit percentages of predicted entities — and they cluster precisely where the model is uncertain, which is to say near boundaries. You have three options:


    
  
  1. Repair on decode. Treat an orphaned I-X as if it were B-X. This is what the Hugging Face aggregation strategies do, and what the decoder in Section 6 does. It never crashes and it recovers a plausible span.
    2. 
  
  2. Constrain the decode. Run Viterbi with −∞ on illegal transitions. Guarantees well-formed output. This is a decode-time change, so you can add it to an already-trained softmax model.
    3. 
  
  3. Train a CRF layer on top. The transition matrix learns the constraints from data, and — more importantly — learns soft preferences too, such as "I-ORG after I-ORG is common, B-PER immediately after B-PER is unusual". Section 9.
    4. 




  The silent version of this bug. A decoder that skips orphaned I- tags instead of repairing them drops entities on the floor. Your recall falls, no exception is raised, and the loss curve looks perfect. Always log how many repairs your decoder performed per thousand tokens; a sudden jump in that counter is an early warning that your model or your input distribution has shifted.





6 · From tags to character spans interactive


A tag sequence is not a deliverable. Nobody's downstream system wants ['O','B-ORG','I-ORG', …]; they want {"text": "European Central Bank", "type": "ORG", "start": 4, "end": 25}, with start and end as offsets into the original string so the UI can highlight the exact characters the user typed.


The decoder is a small state machine. In pseudocode:


current = None
for i, tag in enumerate(tags):
    if tag == "O":
        if current: emit(current); current = None
    elif tag.startswith("B-"):
        if current: emit(current)
        current = Span(type=tag[2:], tok_start=i, tok_end=i)
    else:                                  # tag.startswith("I-")
        if current and current.type == tag[2:]:
            current.tok_end = i            # extend
        else:
            if current: emit(current)
            current = Span(type=tag[2:], tok_start=i, tok_end=i)   # repair
if current: emit(current)

# then map token indices to character offsets
for span in spans:
    span.start = offsets[span.tok_start][0]
    span.end   = offsets[span.tok_end][1]
    span.text  = raw_text[span.start:span.end]


Three details that separate a correct decoder from an almost-correct one:


    
  
  • Flush at the end. If the sentence ends inside an entity, the loop body never runs again. Forgetting the final emit silently loses every entity that touches the end of a document — and documents very often end with a signature or a place name.
    • 
  
  • Offsets come from the tokenizer, not from you. span.text should be a slice of the original string, never a " ".join(tokens). Section 7.
    • 
  
  • End offsets are exclusive. Python slicing, JavaScript slice, and the Hugging Face offset_mapping all use half-open intervals. If your database stores inclusive ends and your UI assumes exclusive, every highlight is one character short and no test catches it until a one-character entity disappears.
    •

7 · Character offsets, done properly interactivecode


Here is the trap, stated plainly: do not reconstruct character offsets by counting characters yourself. Every method that seems reasonable is wrong on real text.



  
MethodHow it fails

  
text.index(token)Finds the first occurrence. The second Zurich in a document maps to the first one. Worse, it matches inside other words: "Nordwind".index("in") succeeds.

  
Cumulative offset += len(tok) + 1Assumes exactly one space between tokens. Two spaces, a tab, a newline, or a non-breaking space and every subsequent offset is wrong — by a growing amount, so late entities are wrong by more than early ones.

  
" ".join(tokens) then index into thatYou are now indexing into a string the user never sent. Highlights in the UI land on the wrong characters.

  
Byte offsets from a UTF-8 encodingPython string indices are character indices. A byte offset into a document containing accented or non-Latin characters points somewhere else entirely.




The correct source of offsets is the tokenizer itself. A Hugging Face fast tokenizer returns them for free:


from transformers import AutoTokenizer
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("bert-base-cased")   # a *fast* tokenizer

enc = tok(text, return_offsets_mapping=True, truncation=True)
# enc["offset_mapping"] -> [(0,0), (0,8), (9,12), ...]   one (start, end) per *sub-token*
# (0,0) marks a special token ([CLS], [SEP]) that maps to no characters

def span_text(text, offsets, i, j):
    return text[offsets[i][0]:offsets[j][1]]           # exact, always


If you are running a CRF on whitespace or spaCy tokens rather than sub-tokens, get offsets from the tokenizer you actually used — spaCy gives you token.idx, and a regex tokenizer gives you m.start() and m.end() from the match object. The rule is the same in all cases: the component that split the text is the only component that knows where the pieces came from.

8 · A CRF baseline: the features interactivecode


A linear-chain CRF scores a whole tag sequence:



  score(x, y) = Σi [ w · f(x, i, yi) + A[yi−1, yi] ]

  P(y | x) = exp(score(x, y)) / Σy′ exp(score(x, y′))



Two pieces: the unary term w · f(x, i, yi), which asks "how well does this tag fit this token in this context", and the transition term A[yi−1, yi], which asks "how well does this tag follow that one". The normaliser sums over all tag sequences, which sounds intractable and is not — the forward algorithm does it in O(n |L|²).


In a neural tagger, f is learned. In a classical CRF, you write it. A standard, honest feature template for a token at position i:


def word2features(sent, i):
    w = sent[i][0]                      # surface form
    postag = sent[i][1]
    feats = {
        "bias": 1.0,
        "w.lower": w.lower(),
        "w[-3:]": w[-3:],               # suffix: "-ing", "-Ltd", "-ton"
        "w[-2:]": w[-2:],
        "w[:2]": w[:2],                 # prefix
        "w.isupper": w.isupper(),       # ACRONYM
        "w.istitle": w.istitle(),       # Capitalised
        "w.isdigit": w.isdigit(),
        "w.shape": shape(w),            # "Xxxxx", "XX", "dddd"
        "pos": postag,
        "pos[:2]": postag[:2],
    }
    if i > 0:
        pw, ppos = sent[i-1][0], sent[i-1][1]
        feats.update({"-1:w.lower": pw.lower(), "-1:w.istitle": pw.istitle(),
                      "-1:pos": ppos})
    else:
        feats["BOS"] = True
    if i < len(sent) - 1:
        nw, npos = sent[i+1][0], sent[i+1][1]
        feats.update({"+1:w.lower": nw.lower(), "+1:w.istitle": nw.istitle(),
                      "+1:pos": npos})
    else:
        feats["EOS"] = True
    return feats


Why each family earns its place:


    
  
  • Suffixes. -ton, -burg, -ville, -stan are location morphology; -Ltd, -GmbH, -Corp, -Inc are organisation morphology. Three characters is usually the sweet spot; the feature generalises to words the model never saw, which is the entire point (Section 13, unseen entities).
    • 
  
  • Prefixes. Weaker than suffixes in English, useful in morphologically richer languages, and cheap.
    • 
  
  • Capitalisation and shape. The single strongest cue in newswire English, and the single most fragile one. It transfers to nothing: all-caps headlines, lowercased search queries, and social media all break it. A model that leans on istitle is a model that will fail your first real customer.
    • 
  
  • Part of speech. A proper noun tag (NNP) is a strong prior for the start of an entity; a verb tag is strong evidence against. POS also disambiguates May the modal from May the month.
    • 
  
  • Neighbour features. A ±1 or ±2 window is what gives the CRF any context at all. This is the concrete thing a transformer replaces: instead of two neighbours you get the whole sentence, weighted by attention.
    •

9 · The transition matrix and Viterbi mathinteractive


Here is the question this section answers: what does the transition matrix actually buy you over taking the argmax at each position independently?


The answer, precisely: it lets a locally-suboptimal tag win when it makes the whole sequence coherent. Independent argmax maximises Σi ui[yi]. Viterbi maximises Σi ui[yi] + A[yi−1, yi]. When A assigns a large negative number to O → I-LOC, a path through I-LOC at a span-initial position is worth less than the same path with B-LOC, even though the unary score preferred I-LOC.


The recursion, in full:



  δ1[k] = u1[k] + A[start, k]

  δt[k] = ut[k] + maxj ( δt−1[j] + A[j, k] )

  ψt[k] = argmaxj ( δt−1[j] + A[j, k] )



Then read the best final state and follow ψ backwards. Cost: O(n |L|²) time, O(n |L|) memory. With |L| = 9 that is nothing; you can afford it on every request.

A real CRF does not use a single penalty knob: every one of the |L|² entries is a learned parameter, and the illegal ones simply end up very negative because no training sequence ever used them.





The transition matrix buys you three distinct things, and it is worth keeping them separate:


    
  
  1. Hard legality. Illegal transitions become impossible. You never emit an orphaned I- again.
    2. 
  
  2. Soft sequence preference. Real transition weights encode style: entities are usually short, I-ORG follows I-ORG readily, and two single-token PER entities side by side are unusual. That is learned from data, not asserted by you.
    3. 
  
  3. Global consistency. Evidence at token 7 can change the tag at token 4, because the path is scored as a whole. A per-token softmax can never do this — the decisions are made independently even though the features were contextual.
    4. 




  Does a CRF layer on top of a transformer help? Less than it used to. A pre-trained encoder already gives every position a representation shaped by the whole sentence, so much of the sequence consistency is baked into the unary scores. The remaining gain is mostly on legality, which you can also get for free with constrained decoding. Add the CRF layer if you measure a gain on your data; do not add it because it appears in every architecture diagram from 2018.





10 · Fine-tuning a transformer token classifier code


The architecture is almost boringly small: take a pre-trained encoder, throw away its pre-training head, and put a single linear layer on top of every position's hidden state.



  H = Encoder(x) ∈ ℝn × d

  logits = H W⊤ + b ∈ ℝn × |L|

  loss = (1/|M|) Σi ∈ M CrossEntropy(logitsi, yi)



W is |L| × d — with BERT-base and nine tags, 9 × 768 + 9 = 6,921 new parameters on top of 108 million pre-trained ones. Almost all of the capability comes from the encoder; the head is a readout.


The set M in the loss is the crucial detail: it is the set of positions that carry a label. Special tokens, padding, and continuation sub-tokens are excluded. In PyTorch that is done with the label value -100, which CrossEntropyLoss ignores by default. Section 11 is entirely about getting M right.


from transformers import AutoModelForTokenClassification, TrainingArguments, Trainer

model = AutoModelForTokenClassification.from_pretrained(
    "bert-base-cased",
    num_labels=len(label_list),
    id2label={i: l for i, l in enumerate(label_list)},   # ship these, or your
    label2id={l: i for i, l in enumerate(label_list)},   # API returns "LABEL_3"
)

args = TrainingArguments(
    output_dir="runs/entityfinder",
    learning_rate=3e-5,          # 2e-5 .. 5e-5 is the usual band for BERT-base
    per_device_train_batch_size=16,
    num_train_epochs=3,          # NER converges fast; 5+ usually overfits
    weight_decay=0.01,
    eval_strategy="epoch",
    seed=13,
)


Practical notes that save a day each:


    
  
  • Cased, not uncased. Capitalisation is a real signal for names. Starting from bert-base-uncased throws it away before you begin.
    • 
  
  • Ship id2label in the config. If you skip it, the model's own outputs come back as LABEL_0 … LABEL_8 and the mapping lives only in a notebook cell you will lose.
    • 
  
  • Record the model version with every prediction. A prediction row without a model version cannot be re-evaluated after you retrain, and a review queue full of unattributable predictions is a review queue you have to throw away.
    • 
  
  • Do not shuffle across the document boundary when splitting. Newswire corpora repeat the same entities inside a story. If sentences from one story land in both train and test, you have leaked, and your test F1 measures memorisation.
    • 
  
  • Sub-tokenise before you decide on truncation. A 200-word sentence can exceed 512 sub-tokens with a cased vocabulary on unusual names. Truncated tail = silently missing entities.
    • 



Reference material worth reading in full: the token-classification chapter of the Hugging Face course at huggingface.co/learn/nlp-course/chapter7/2, and Chapter 8 of Speech and Language Processing at web.stanford.edu/~jurafsky/slp3/8.pdf. For the classical baseline, the sklearn-crfsuite documentation at sklearn-crfsuite.readthedocs.io is short and complete. 11 · The subword alignment problem interactive


Your labels are per word. Your model consumes sub-tokens. One word becomes one, two, or five pieces, and the two sequences no longer line up.


words:      Nordwind          Air    opened        in    Cape   Town
labels:     B-ORG             I-ORG  O             O     B-LOC  I-LOC

sub-tokens: [CLS] Nord ##wind Air    open  ##ed    in    Cape   To  ##wn  [SEP]
            ^^^^^ 11 positions, 6 labels — these cannot be zipped together


The standard rule: label the first sub-token of each word; set every other position to -100. Special tokens get -100 too. The loss then ignores continuations, and at inference you read the prediction from each word's first piece.


def align_labels(words, labels, tokenizer):
    enc = tokenizer(words, is_split_into_words=True, truncation=True)
    word_ids = enc.word_ids()            # e.g. [None, 0, 0, 1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 5, 5, None]
    out, prev = [], None
    for wid in word_ids:
        if wid is None:                  # [CLS], [SEP], padding
            out.append(-100)
        elif wid != prev:                # first piece of a new word
            out.append(label2id[labels[wid]])
        else:                            # continuation piece
            out.append(-100)
        prev = wid
    enc["labels"] = out
    return enc


word_ids() is the whole answer, and it only exists on a fast tokenizer. If tokenizer.is_fast is False, stop and fix that before writing another line.


An acceptable variant is to label every piece of a word, converting B- to I- on continuations. It changes the loss slightly (long words contribute more) and it decodes identically as long as you still read the first piece. What is not acceptable is zipping the label list onto the sub-token list positionally, or forgetting to skip [CLS]. Both produce a label sequence that is off by a growing amount, and both train without error.

  Why this is the most expensive bug in the topic. Broken alignment does not crash, does not warn, and does not make the loss diverge. The model dutifully learns a corrupted mapping and reports a plausible training loss. You find out at evaluation, when entity F1 comes back near zero — or worse, you do not find out, because you evaluated with the same broken alignment on both sides and the errors cancelled. Sanity check by decoding your training labels back into spans and printing them next to the original text for ten random sentences. If they do not match, nothing downstream matters.





12 · Entity-level vs token-level precision, recall, and F1 mathinteractive


NER is scored at the entity level. A prediction counts as a true positive only if the type is right and both boundaries are right. Anything else is one false positive plus one false negative — partial credit does not exist.



  P = TP / (TP + FP) = TP / (number of predicted entities)

  R = TP / (TP + FN) = TP / (number of gold entities)

  F1 = 2PR / (P + R)



Token-level metrics compute the same three numbers over individual tokens instead of spans. They are not wrong as a diagnostic — they tell you whether the model knows the type — but reporting them as your headline number inflates your result, because a boundary error that costs you two entities costs you only one or two tokens.



A worked example, by hand


Two sentences. Gold above, model prediction below.

13 · Error categories worth naming


"F1 = 0.83" tells you nothing actionable. A breakdown by error category tells you what to build next. Three categories cover most of what you will see, and your topic report needs one worked example of each.



Boundary errors


Right type, wrong edges. Sub-cases: too short (Cape for Cape Town), too long (the European Central Bank including the determiner), split (one entity emitted as two), merged (two adjacent entities emitted as one).


text:  ... a flight operated by Nordwind Air Ltd from Cape Town ...
gold:  ORG "Nordwind Air Ltd"        LOC "Cape Town"
pred:  ORG "Nordwind Air"            LOC "Cape"
       ^ dropped the corporate suffix     ^ dropped the head noun


Cause: the annotation guideline includes tokens the model has no local evidence for. Fixes that actually work: BIOES tagging (an explicit end class), more training examples containing the suffix in question, and a post-processing gazetteer of corporate suffixes for the specific, enumerable cases.



Type confusions


Right span, wrong label. The classic pairs are ORG ⇄ LOC (metonymy: Zurich raised fares), PER ⇄ ORG (companies named after founders), and MISC ⇄ everything (because MISC is defined by exclusion and is therefore incoherent).


text:  Zurich said the fare increase would take effect on Tuesday .
gold:  ORG "Zurich"      (the operator, by this corpus's guideline)
pred:  LOC "Zurich"      (the city, by every other piece of evidence)


Cause: genuine ambiguity resolved by a guideline convention. Fix: more in-domain examples of the convention, and a per-type confusion matrix in your report so a reviewer can see that the model is not confused about entities, only about this pair.



Unseen entities


Names absent from training. This is the honest test of generalisation, and it is where the CRF and the transformer separate most clearly. A CRF that learned the string Frankfurt knows nothing about Ouagadougou; a CRF that learned suffix and shape features has a chance. A transformer has sub-word representations and a language model's worth of prior exposure, so it degrades far more gracefully.


text:  The delegation flew to Ouagadougou on Tuesday .
CRF:   O O O O O O O            (no lexical feature fires; shape alone was not enough)
BERT:  ... B-LOC ...            (sub-tokens + "flew to" context)



  Measure it deliberately. Split your test entities into seen in training and unseen, and report F1 for each. A model at 0.90 overall might be at 0.94 on seen entities and 0.61 on unseen ones — and since every new document contains new names, the 0.61 is the number that predicts production behaviour.





14 · Domain shift, or why the newswire model disappoints


Almost every off-the-shelf English NER model is trained on newswire from a narrow period. Newswire is edited: sentences are well-formed, capitalisation is consistent, names are introduced in full before being shortened, and the entity types were chosen to suit that text. Your documents are none of those things.



  
Target domainWhat breaksWhat to do first

  
Clinical notesTelegraphic syntax, heavy abbreviation, entity types (drug, dose, finding) that simply are not in the label setRelabel. A newswire model cannot invent a type it was never trained on. Start from a domain-pre-trained encoder.

  
Legal documentsVery long entities (party names, statute citations), defined terms in quotes, capitalisation used for emphasisCustom types plus rule-based extraction for citations, which are regular and better handled by a regex than by a model.

  
Support tickets and chatLowercase everything, typos, product names that look like common nounsTrain on lowercased data or augment with case-perturbed copies. Add a product gazetteer.

  
Social mediaNo case signal, hashtags, usernames, emoji, code-switchingA domain-specific model. Newswire transfer is weak enough here that fine-tuning on a few thousand in-domain sentences beats any amount of prompt engineering.

  
OCR'd scansCharacter-level noise inside entity strings, broken line structureFix the OCR first. Sub-word models are somewhat robust to noise, but nothing recovers a name that was never legible.




The cheapest useful experiment in this whole topic: take 100 sentences from your real target domain, annotate them yourself, and evaluate the off-the-shelf model on them. It costs an afternoon and it tells you whether you are looking at a fine-tuning project or a data-collection project. Do it before you write the training loop, not after.


One consistent finding across domain-transfer work is worth internalising: case sensitivity is the most brittle feature you can rely on. A model trained on properly-cased newswire and evaluated on lowercased text loses a large fraction of its F1 — not because it forgot what an organisation is, but because it was quietly using capitalisation as most of its evidence. If your production text is not reliably cased, train on text that is not reliably cased.




15 · A softmax value is not a probability of correctness mathinteractive


Your model returns 0.94 for an entity. What does that number mean?


Mechanically, it means: after the linear head produced nine logits and the softmax normalised them, the winning class received 0.94 of the mass. That is a statement about the shape of nine numbers. It is not a measurement of how often predictions like this one turn out to be right, and it becomes one only if you check.



  softmax(z)k = exp(zk) / Σj exp(zj)



Three reasons the number is optimistic:


    
  
  • Training pushes it up. Cross-entropy is minimised by putting all the mass on the gold class. A model trained to convergence is trained to be confident, including where it should not be.
    • 
  
  • It is a token-level number, and your product ships entities. An entity's confidence has to be aggregated from its tokens — usually the minimum or the mean. The minimum is more conservative and usually the better choice for a review queue; whichever you pick, write it in the model card, because the two produce visibly different queues.
    • 
  
  • It says nothing about the things not predicted. A missed entity has no confidence score at all. Thresholding on confidence filters false positives; it cannot surface false negatives. That asymmetry is structural and no threshold fixes it.
    • 



The check is a reliability diagram: bin predictions by confidence, and in each bin compare mean confidence against observed accuracy. A calibrated model sits on the diagonal. The summary number is expected calibration error:



  ECE = Σb (nb / N) · | acc(b) − conf(b) |





  
Demo 9 · Reliability diagram over 48 reviewed predictions

  
These 48 entity predictions were each checked by a human, so we know the truth. Change the bin count and watch the diagram and the ECE recompute from the same data.

  

    

      

    

  

  
  

  
Watch what happens with 10 bins: some bins hold two or three items and their accuracy jumps to 0 or 1. A reliability diagram from a small sample is mostly noise. Bin coarsely, or collect more reviewed data.





If the diagram bows below the diagonal — accuracy consistently under confidence — the model is overconfident, which is the normal case. Fixes, in increasing order of effort: temperature scaling (divide the logits by a single scalar T > 1 fitted on a held-out set — one parameter, no retraining, and it never changes the argmax), label smoothing during training, or an ensemble. Temperature scaling is almost always the right first move.



  Calibrating does not make the model more accurate. It makes the number attached to each prediction mean what it says. That is exactly what a review queue needs, because a queue is built by comparing that number to a threshold.





16 · The review queue as a product pattern interactive


No NER system is right all the time, so the question is not "how do we avoid errors" but "where do the errors go". The review queue is the standard answer: predictions below a confidence threshold are held for a human, everything above it is auto-accepted.


The threshold is a business decision expressed as a number:


    
  
  • Raise it → more predictions queued → more errors caught → more reviewer hours.
    • 
  
  • Lower it → fewer reviewer hours → more errors flow straight into the downstream system.
    • 



There is no correct threshold, only a defensible one. Defend it with the curve.

The widget computes the review workload, the errors caught, the errors that got through, and the precision of what you shipped without looking at it.

  

    

      

    

      

    

  

  
  





What the queue has to store


A review decision is only worth capturing if it can be turned back into training data. That means the row must keep both the original prediction and the human's correction, never overwrite one with the other:


entity_predictions
  id, document_hash, model_version, predicted_at
  char_start, char_end, entity_type, surface_text, confidence

entity_reviews
  id, prediction_id  -> entity_predictions.id
  decision            'accept' | 'correct' | 'reject'
  corrected_type, corrected_start, corrected_end       -- null on accept
  reviewer_id, reviewed_at


Design notes that matter more than they look:


    
  
  • Two tables, not one. If the reviewer's correction overwrites the prediction, you have destroyed the evidence you needed to measure the model. Append, never mutate.
    • 
  
  • Store the document hash, not the document. You can still group, deduplicate, and audit; you are not accumulating a copy of every text anyone pasted in. For sensitive domains this is the difference between a defensible design and an incident.
    • 
  
  • Store the model version on the prediction row. Without it you cannot tell whether last month's errors came from the model you are running today.
    • 
  
  • Reviewed items are gold. Corrections are annotated data, produced by exactly the distribution shift you care about. Feeding them back is the cheapest accuracy improvement available — but note that they are a biased sample (only low-confidence items were reviewed), so use them for training, not for measuring.
    • 
  
  • The queue is also your unseen-entity monitor. A sudden change in queue volume usually means the input distribution moved, not that the model got worse. Chart it.
    • 





  Putting it together. An extraction service that meets this topic's bar: a fine-tuned transformer token classifier and a CRF baseline behind one API; offsets taken from the fast tokenizer and never recomputed; entity-level micro and macro P/R/F1 reported per type for both models; every extraction persisted with its character span, type, confidence, model version, and timestamp; a threshold-driven review queue whose decisions are appended, not overwritten; and a model card that states the training domain, the aggregation rule for entity confidence, the threshold, and the three error categories you actually measured.
