Topic 2 — Natural Language Understanding as Text Classification

From a bag of words and a TF-IDF matrix to a fine-tuned encoder transformer, and then the harder half of the job: deciding whether the number you printed actually means anything.

What you should walk away with. (1) Able to build a bag-of-words vector by hand and state precisely what it discards. (2) Able to compute tf, idf and tf–idf for a term in a document with a calculator and nothing else. (3) A clear mental picture of a linear decision boundary in a very high-dimensional sparse space. (4) Able to say what a pretrained encoder contributes that no reweighting of counts can. (5) Fluent with fine-tuning mechanics: frozen versus unfrozen layers, learning rate, epoch count, and the shape of overfitting on a small corpus. (6) Able to read a 2×2 confusion matrix and derive accuracy, precision, recall and F1 without looking anything up. (7) Able to explain, with numbers, why accuracy is a misleading headline under class imbalance. (8) Convinced that a softmax score is not a probability of being correct, and able to measure the gap.
Scope note. Everything in these notes is framed for single-label binary classification: every example carries exactly one label, and that label takes one of exactly two values. That is what the topic project (Classify-It) requires. Multiclass (more than two label values) and multilabel (more than one label per example) change the definitions of precision, recall and F1 in ways that need their own treatment, and they are out of scope for this course. When you read an external tutorial that averages F1 over classes, notice that it is solving a different problem than yours.

1 · "Understanding" when the output is a label

Natural language understanding is a broad phrase, and broad phrases are hard to grade. This topic pins it to something measurable: a function that takes a span of text and returns one of two labels, plus a number saying how sure it is.

f : text  →  (label ∈ {0, 1},  confidence ∈ [0, 1])

That looks like a reduction, and it is. But most deployed NLU sits exactly here. "Is this support message about billing or not?" "Is this review negative?" "Does this survey comment contain a safety concern?" Each is a binary decision that a system has to make thousands of times a day, and each is a place where the difference between a model that reads context and a model that counts words shows up in real money.

Two model families dominate, and this topic teaches both because you cannot judge the second without the first:

  • The classical pipeline. Turn text into a sparse vector of weighted word counts (TF–IDF), then fit a linear classifier (logistic regression). Trains in seconds on a laptop, needs no GPU, and every weight is a word you can read.
  • The fine-tuned encoder. Take a transformer encoder that has already been pretrained on a large corpus, bolt a small classification head on top, and continue training the whole thing on your labeled data. Costs orders of magnitude more compute and gives you almost no direct interpretability, but it has read a lot of language before it ever sees your task.

The interesting engineering question is never "which is better in general." It is "for my corpus, at my label count, under my latency budget, is the second one enough better to justify what it costs?" You answer that with a held-out split and four metrics, and you answer it honestly only if you understand what those metrics hide.

A framing trap. People say "the transformer understands the sentence." A fine-tuned classifier does not understand anything in the way that word implies; it has learned a mapping from token sequences to a label that happens to generalize better than word counts do. When it fails, it fails in ways that have nothing to do with meaning — it will happily latch onto a spurious cue, such as the fact that in your corpus every complaint happens to contain the word "order". Naming the mechanism instead of the metaphor keeps you honest about where it will break.

2 · Bag of words, and what it throws away interactive

A classifier needs numbers. The oldest way to get them from text is the bag-of-words representation:

  1. Fix a vocabulary V — every distinct term you are willing to count.
  2. Represent each document as a vector of length |V|.
  3. Entry j is the number of times term j occurs in that document.

Take "the food was cold and the service was slow". With a vocabulary drawn from that sentence, the vector is:

and=1  cold=1  food=1  service=1  slow=1  the=2  was=2

Everything else in V is zero. That is the defining property of this representation: a real vocabulary has 20,000 to 200,000 entries, a real document has 15 to 300 tokens, so more than 99.9% of every vector is zero. This is why the representation is called sparse, and why it is stored as a list of (index, value) pairs rather than a dense array.

What survives, and what does not

The name is exact. Pour the words of a document into a bag and shake it. What survives is which words occurred and how often. What is destroyed:

  • Order. the dog bit the man and the man bit the dog have identical vectors. Every permutation of a document maps to the same point.
  • Negation scope. not bad, actually good and not good, actually bad are identical. The word not is present in both; nothing records what it attaches to.
  • Syntax. Subject and object, main clause and subordinate clause, question and statement — none of it is encoded.
  • Synonymy. refund and reimbursement are two unrelated coordinates. A model that learned a weight for one has learned nothing about the other.
  • Morphology, unless you intervene. refund, refunded and refunds are three separate coordinates. Stemming or lemmatizing collapses them, at the cost of some precision.
  • Word sense. bank as a river edge and bank as a financial institution share one coordinate and therefore one weight.

Read that list again with the topic project in mind, because it is the complete case for using something else. It is also the reason the classical pipeline is not a strawman: for many corpora the label is genuinely predictable from which words appear, and then the discarded information was not needed.

The tokenizer is part of the model

"Count the words" hides at least five decisions, and each one changes the vectors:

  • Case folding. Are Refund and refund the same term? Usually yes; but folding case destroys the signal in ALL CAPS complaints.
  • Punctuation and emoji. Stripping them is the default. In sentiment corpora that is often a mistake — !!! carries information.
  • Stop words. Dropping the, is, of shrinks the vocabulary. It also removes function words that matter for some tasks, and as section 3 shows, idf already handles ubiquitous terms without a hand-written list.
  • n-grams. Counting adjacent pairs (not good as a single term) restores a sliver of word order. It also multiplies the vocabulary size and the sparsity.
  • Minimum document frequency. Terms occurring in one document out of 5,000 are mostly noise and typos; min_df=2 is a common floor.

Record every one of these choices in your project's configuration. A TF–IDF baseline whose tokenizer settings are not written down is not reproducible, and an irreproducible baseline is worthless as a point of comparison.

3 · Term frequency, document frequency, TF–IDF mathinteractive

Raw counts have an obvious defect: the most frequent terms in every document are the least informative ones. the occurs twice in the example above, more than any content word. If you feed raw counts to a classifier, the largest feature values carry the least signal.

TF–IDF fixes this with a product of two factors.

Term frequency

How much of this document is this term? The simplest form is the raw count; the version used throughout these notes normalizes by document length so a 400-word document does not dominate a 20-word one:

tf(t, d) = count(t, d) / |d|

where |d| is the number of tokens in d. Two other forms you will meet: raw count, and log-scaled 1 + log(count). All three are monotone in the count; they differ in how quickly the value saturates.

Inverse document frequency

How rare is this term across the corpus? Let N be the number of documents and df(t) the number of documents containing t at least once (the count within a document is irrelevant here — this is a document count, not a term count):

idf(t) = log( N / df(t) )

Read the two limits and the whole idea falls out:

  • A term in every document has df = N, so idf = log(1) = 0. Its tf–idf is zero in every document. It cannot influence any decision. This is idf doing automatically and continuously what a stop-word list does crudely and discretely.
  • A term in one document out of N has idf = log(N), the maximum. Rarity is treated as informativeness.

The base of the logarithm is a scale factor on every idf at once. It cannot change the ranking of terms, and for a linear model it is absorbed by the learned weights. These notes use base 10 so the arithmetic is checkable on any calculator; scikit-learn uses the natural log.

The product

tfidf(t, d) = tf(t, d) × idf(t)

A term scores high in a document only if it is frequent here and rare elsewhere. Frequent everywhere gets zeroed by idf; rare here gets a small tf. This is the whole of the classical representation.

Two things that trip people up in implementation. First, scikit-learn's TfidfVectorizer does not use the textbook formula — by default it computes a smoothed idf(t) = ln((1 + N) / (1 + df(t))) + 1, which is never zero, and it then L2-normalizes each document vector. Second, idf must be computed on the training split only. Call fit_transform on train and transform on test. Fitting the vectorizer on the full corpus leaks test-set document frequencies into training and inflates every number you report.

4 · A TF–IDF computation done fully by hand math

Do this one with a calculator before you rely on a library. The corpus is four documents, N = 4:

d1: the service was slow and the food was cold
d2: the food was excellent and the service was fast
d3: slow service ruined the meal
d4: excellent meal and excellent service

Step 1 — tokenize d1 and count

Lowercase, split on whitespace. d1 has 9 tokens: the, service, was, slow, and, the, food, was, cold. Seven distinct terms:

the = 2    service = 1    was = 2    slow = 1
and = 1    food = 1       cold = 1        →  |d1| = 9

Step 2 — document frequencies over the whole corpus

For each term of d1, count how many of the four documents contain it at least once. Note that the occurs twice in d1 and twice in d2, and that changes nothing here: df counts documents.

the      → d1, d2, d3            → df = 3
service  → d1, d2, d3, d4        → df = 4
was      → d1, d2                → df = 2
slow     → d1, d3                → df = 2
and      → d1, d2, d4            → df = 3
food     → d1, d2                → df = 2
cold     → d1                    → df = 1

Step 3 — idf, base 10

idf(the)     = log10(4/3) = log10(1.3333) = 0.1249
idf(service) = log10(4/4) = log10(1.0000) = 0.0000
idf(was)     = log10(4/2) = log10(2.0000) = 0.3010
idf(slow)    = log10(4/2) = 0.3010
idf(and)     = log10(4/3) = 0.1249
idf(food)    = log10(4/2) = 0.3010
idf(cold)    = log10(4/1) = log10(4)      = 0.6021

service occurs in all four documents, so its idf is exactly zero and its tf–idf will be zero in every document. No stop-word list was consulted; the statistic did the work.

Step 4 — tf, normalized by |d1| = 9

tf(the)  = 2/9 = 0.2222      tf(was)     = 2/9 = 0.2222
tf(slow) = 1/9 = 0.1111      tf(service) = 1/9 = 0.1111
tf(and)  = 1/9 = 0.1111      tf(food)    = 1/9 = 0.1111
tf(cold) = 1/9 = 0.1111

Step 5 — multiply

termcounttf = count/9dfidf = log10(4/df)tf–idf
cold10.111110.60210.0669
was20.222220.30100.0669
slow10.111120.30100.0334
food10.111120.30100.0334
the20.222230.12490.0278
and10.111130.12490.0139
service10.111140.00000.0000

Three observations worth more than the arithmetic:

  • the is the most frequent term in d1 and lands fifth of seven. Raw counts would have ranked it first.
  • cold occurs once and ties for the top score, because it is the only term unique to this document. In a complaint classifier that is exactly the term you want weighted heavily.
  • was also scores 0.0669, and it is a function word carrying no sentiment. TF–IDF is a frequency statistic, not a semantic one: it rewards distinctiveness within this corpus, and in a four-document corpus was happens to be distinctive. With 5,000 documents, was would appear nearly everywhere and its idf would fall toward zero. Small corpora produce unstable idf values; this is one concrete reason the topic project requires at least 5,000 rows.
Check yourself. Compute tf–idf for excellent in d4 before reading on. d4 = excellent meal and excellent service, so |d4| = 5 and count(excellent, d4) = 2, giving tf = 0.4000. excellent appears in d2 and d4, so df = 2 and idf = log10(2) = 0.3010. The product is 0.1204 — the largest tf–idf value anywhere in this corpus, which is the correct outcome: the term is repeated in a short document and absent from half the corpus.

5 · Logistic regression on sparse features mathinteractive

You now have, for each document, a vector x of length |V| that is almost all zeros. Logistic regression scores it with a dot product and squashes the result:

z = w · x + b = Σj wj xj + b
p(y = 1 | x) = σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z)

Three properties of the sigmoid are worth memorizing: σ(0) = 0.5; σ is strictly increasing, so ranking by z and ranking by p are the same ranking; and σ(−z) = 1 − σ(z). The last one is why binary classification needs only one score.

Training minimizes binary cross-entropy over the labeled examples:

L = −(1/n) Σi [ yi log pi + (1 − yi) log(1 − pi) ] + λ ||w||2

A useful sanity number: a model that outputs 0.5 for everything has a loss of −log(0.5) = 0.693. That value shows up again in section 10 as the starting point of every fine-tuning curve, and if your training loss is sitting at 0.693 after several epochs, your model has learned nothing at all.

What the decision boundary is

The set of points where p = 0.5 is exactly the set where z = 0, which is the hyperplane w · x + b = 0. In two dimensions it is a line; in a 30,000-dimensional TF–IDF space it is a 29,999-dimensional hyperplane. You cannot picture that, but three facts carry over from the picture you can draw:

  • The boundary is flat. No curvature. If the two classes are not separable by a flat surface in this feature space, no amount of training fixes it — you have to change the features.
  • w is perpendicular to the boundary, and its magnitude controls how sharply p swings from 0 to 1 as you cross. Large weights mean confident predictions near the boundary; the L2 penalty λ exists to hold them down.
  • Because x is sparse, z is a sum over only the terms actually present in the document — typically a few dozen products, not 30,000. That is why the classical pipeline serves a prediction in microseconds.

The weights are readable, which is the real selling point

Each coordinate of w belongs to one term. Sort w and you get the model's evidence in plain words:

most positive weights (push toward label 1 = complaint)
   +2.91  refund        +2.44  broken       +2.02  cancelled
   +1.88  never         +1.77  charged      +1.61  unacceptable

most negative weights (push toward label 0 = not a complaint)
   -2.55  thanks        -2.10  perfect      -1.94  helpful
   -1.72  quick         -1.55  resolved     -1.39  appreciate

Two caveats before you put a list like this in a report. Weight magnitudes are comparable across terms only if the features are on a comparable scale — with L2-normalized TF–IDF they roughly are, with raw counts they are not. And a large weight on a term that occurs in nine training documents is memorization, not evidence; check the document frequency before you believe any single row.

6 · Where the sparse linear model is blind

The failures below are not bugs and not a shortage of training data. They follow from the representation, and no amount of extra labeled examples removes them.

Input pairWhat the model seesWhy it cannot recover
not bad at all vs not good at allBoth contain not; the sentiment word differs, so the vectors differ — but the model has learned independent weights for not, bad and good and adds them.The effect of not is multiplicative on what follows. A sum of independent weights cannot express "flip the sign of the next term".
not bad, actually good vs not good, actually badIdentical vectors.Bag of words is permutation-invariant. Two inputs mapping to one point must receive one prediction.
I would like a refund vs I already got my refundShares the high-weight term refund.Tense and aspect are carried by function words the pipeline often discards, and by structure it never encodes.
the charge was reversed vs the reversal was chargedDifferent surface forms of the same stems; after stemming, near-identical vectors.Argument structure — who did what to whom — lives in syntax.
my order never arrived vs my parcel did not turn upAlmost no vocabulary overlap.Synonymy is invisible: every term is an orthogonal coordinate. Weight learned for order transfers nothing to parcel.
sarcastic praise ("great, another outage")great carries a strong negative-class weight.Sarcasm is a pragmatic inversion signalled by context and world knowledge, neither of which is in the vector.

Partial mitigations exist and you should know them, because they are cheap and they sometimes close most of the gap: bigrams turn not good into a single feature; character n-grams give some robustness to spelling; a negation-marking preprocessing pass rewrites tokens after not up to the next punctuation as NOT_token. Each buys back a slice of what the representation discarded, and each inflates the vocabulary. None of them gives the model any knowledge of language that is not already in your labeled corpus. That is the line the next section crosses.

7 · What a pretrained encoder gives you math

An encoder transformer such as BERT or DistilBERT arrives having already consumed a very large volume of running text through a self-supervised objective — masked-language modeling: hide a token, predict it from both sides of the context. Nobody labeled that corpus. The supervision came from the text itself.

What that pretraining leaves behind is a stack of weights that maps a token sequence to a sequence of context-dependent vectors. Four differences from TF–IDF matter for your classifier, and it is worth being precise about each rather than saying "it understands more".

7.1 The representation is contextual

In TF–IDF, the coordinate for bank has one value determined by counts, and therefore one learned weight, no matter which sense is meant. In an encoder, the vector at the position of bank is a function of the entire sentence:

"I sat on the river bank"        →  h_bank = [ ... ]
"I deposited it at the bank"     →  h_bank = [ ... ]   # a different vector

Same surface token, different representation, because self-attention mixed in the surrounding positions. Word order is part of the input via positional information, so the dog bit the man and the man bit the dog produce different hidden states — the collision that bag of words cannot escape does not arise.

7.2 The representation is dense and shared

A hidden state is typically 768 numbers, all of them non-zero, rather than 30,000 numbers of which 20 are non-zero. Because the space is shared and continuous, similar meanings sit near each other. refund and reimbursement are not orthogonal coordinates; they are nearby points. A weight the head learns for one region of that space transfers to inputs the classifier never saw in training. This is the single mechanism behind most of the accuracy gap on small corpora.

7.3 Subword tokenization removes the unknown-word problem

A TF–IDF vocabulary is closed: a term absent from the training corpus is dropped at inference and contributes nothing. Encoders use subword vocabularies (WordPiece, byte-pair encoding), so any string is representable:

"unrefundable"   →  ["un", "##ref", "##und", "##able"]
"Wrigglesworth"  →  ["wr", "##igg", "##les", "##worth"]

Misspellings, product codes and rare compounds degrade gracefully into pieces the model has seen, instead of vanishing.

7.4 The knowledge was paid for once, by somebody else

Pretraining is expensive. Downloading the result is free. Your fine-tuning run starts from weights that already encode a great deal about how English sentences are built, and it only has to learn the last part — how this label depends on that structure. This is why a fine-tuned encoder can beat a TF–IDF baseline with 1,000 labeled examples: it is not learning language from your 1,000 examples, it is learning a task on top of language it already has.

What the encoder does not give you. It does not give you interpretability — there is no list of weights per word to read. It does not give you speed: expect several orders of magnitude more compute per prediction than a dot product over a sparse vector, and a cold-start model load measured in seconds. It does not give you immunity to shortcut learning; a fine-tuned encoder will exploit a spurious correlation in your labels at least as eagerly as a linear model, and it will hide the fact better. And it does not give you calibrated probabilities — see section 14.

The fixed input length

Encoders have a maximum sequence length (512 tokens for BERT-family models, and most fine-tuning setups truncate to 128 or 256 for speed). Text past the cutoff is discarded outright. If your documents are long and the signal sits at the end, truncation quietly destroys it. Measure your token-length distribution before choosing max_length; do not pick 128 because a tutorial did.

8 · Transfer learning: what fine-tuning changes mathinteractive

Fine-tuning is ordinary gradient descent with an unusual starting point. Concretely:

  1. Load pretrained encoder weights.
  2. Discard the pretraining head (the masked-language-model output layer) — you do not need to predict tokens.
  3. Attach a fresh, randomly initialized classification head with two output units.
  4. Train on your labeled data with a small learning rate for a small number of epochs.
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForSequenceClassification

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("distilbert-base-uncased")
model = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(
    "distilbert-base-uncased",
    num_labels=2,                      # single-label binary
    id2label={0: "not_complaint", 1: "complaint"},
    label2id={"not_complaint": 0, "complaint": 1},
)
# The warning about newly initialized classifier weights is expected and correct:
# the head did not exist before this call.

That warning on the first load is not an error. It is the library telling you that pre_classifier and classifier were created from scratch. If you see it while loading your own fine-tuned checkpoint, something is wrong — you saved or referenced the wrong directory.

Frozen versus unfrozen

Every parameter can be trainable or held fixed. The two ends of the spectrum:

Feature extraction (encoder frozen)Full fine-tuning (everything trainable)
What updatesThe classification head onlyHead and all encoder layers
Effective modelLogistic regression on frozen embeddingsThe encoder itself reshapes for your task
Compute per stepForward pass full, backward pass tinyFull forward and backward
MemoryLow — optimizer state for ~0.6M parametersHigh — optimizer state for all ~67M
Data neededWorks with a few hundred examplesWants a few thousand or strong regularization
Typical outcomeBetter than TF–IDF, worse than full fine-tuningBest accuracy; overfits fastest

The middle of the spectrum — unfreeze the top k encoder layers, leave the bottom ones fixed — is the useful default when data is scarce. The justification is empirical but consistent: lower layers of a pretrained encoder encode general lexical and syntactic regularities that your task does not need to change, while upper layers encode more task-shaped structure. Freezing the bottom keeps the general knowledge intact and cuts the number of parameters that can memorize your small training set.

# Freeze the embedding table and the bottom 4 of DistilBERT's 6 layers
for p in model.distilbert.embeddings.parameters():
    p.requires_grad = False
for layer in model.distilbert.transformer.layer[:4]:
    for p in layer.parameters():
        p.requires_grad = False

trainable = sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad)
total     = sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters())
print(f"{trainable:,} / {total:,} trainable ({100*trainable/total:.1f}%)")

The learning rate is not the one you use elsewhere


Training a network from scratch commonly uses learning rates around 10−3. Fine-tuning uses 1e-5 to 5e-5, one to two orders of magnitude smaller, and the reason is structural: the encoder weights are already good. A large step destroys pretrained structure faster than the task signal can rebuild it — the failure mode has a name, catastrophic forgetting, and on a small corpus it looks like a model that trains to a loss of 0.693 and stays there. The head, being randomly initialized, could tolerate a larger rate; a common refinement is a discriminative schedule with a higher rate on the head and progressively smaller rates down the stack.




9 · The classification head mathinteractive


The head is the only part of the model that knows your task exists. For a DistilBERT-style sequence classifier it is four operations:


tokens        → encoder → H  (T × 768)      one vector per token
pool          → h = H[0]     (768,)            the [CLS] position
pre_classifier→ a = ReLU(W1 h + b1)   (768,)    768 × 768
dropout       → a' = dropout(a, p=0.2)         training only
classifier    → z = W2 a' + b2       (2,)      768 × 2  → two logits
softmax       → p = softmax(z)       (2,)      sums to 1



Pooling: which vector represents the document


The encoder returns one vector per token; the head needs one vector per document. Three options in common use:


    
  
  • [CLS] pooling. Use the hidden state at the special first position. BERT was pretrained with an objective that pushes sentence-level information into this position, so it is a reasonable default and it is what the Hugging Face sequence-classification classes do.
    • 
  
  • Mean pooling. Average the hidden states over real tokens, excluding padding. Often slightly better for sentence-similarity work; you must apply the attention mask, or padding positions corrupt the average.
    • 
  
  • Max pooling. Element-wise maximum. Rare for classification.
    • 




Two logits, one decision


For binary classification the head emits two logits and softmax converts them to probabilities:


p1 = ez1 / (ez0 + ez1) = σ(z1 − z0)


The identity on the right is worth internalizing. A two-class softmax is a sigmoid on the difference of the logits, which means only the difference carries information. Add 100 to both logits and nothing changes. Two consequences: the raw magnitude of a logit tells you nothing on its own, and you could equally have built the head with a single output unit. The two-unit form exists so that the same code path handles more classes; for a binary task it is redundant, not wrong.


The loss is cross-entropy on the correct class, L = −log py. Note what the library expects: pass logits to the loss, never softmax outputs. CrossEntropyLoss applies log-softmax internally, and applying softmax twice is a silent bug that produces a model which trains slowly and predicts weakly rather than one that crashes.

10 · Learning rate, epochs, overfitting interactive


Fine-tuning has few dials and they interact. The three that decide whether your run works:



  
DialUsual settingFailure at the low endFailure at the high end

  
learning rate2e-5 – 5e-5Loss barely moves; the run looks like an untrained modelLoss spikes or sticks at 0.693; pretrained structure is destroyed

  
epochs2 – 4Underfit; validation loss still falling when you stopTraining loss → 0 while validation loss climbs

  
batch size16 – 32Noisy gradient estimates, unstable curvesOut of GPU memory; also fewer updates per epoch




Two or three epochs looks absurdly small next to the hundreds you may have used for a network trained from scratch. It is not a typo. The encoder is nearly right when it starts, so there is little to learn and a lot to break; past three or four passes over a small corpus, a 67-million-parameter model begins memorizing individual training examples.



The shape of overfitting


Overfitting is a divergence between two curves, and you can only see it if you plot both:


    
  
  • Training loss falls, validation loss falls. Still learning. Keep going.
    • 
  
  • Training loss falls, validation loss flat. At capacity for this data. Stopping here is reasonable.
    • 
  
  • Training loss falls, validation loss rises. Memorizing. The best checkpoint was the epoch before the turn.
    • 
  
  • Neither moves. Learning rate wrong (usually too high), labels shuffled, or the encoder is frozen when you thought it was not.
    • 



The practical counter-measures, in order of how much they buy you: evaluate every epoch and keep the best validation checkpoint (load_best_model_at_end=True with metric_for_best_model); freeze the lower layers; lower the learning rate; add weight decay around 0.01; increase dropout in the head; get more labeled data. Note that the first item costs nothing and is the one people skip.


from transformers import TrainingArguments

args = TrainingArguments(
    output_dir="runs/classify-it",
    learning_rate=3e-5,
    per_device_train_batch_size=16,
    num_train_epochs=3,
    weight_decay=0.01,
    eval_strategy="epoch",
    save_strategy="epoch",
    load_best_model_at_end=True,
    metric_for_best_model="f1",
    seed=42,                       # record this; without it the run is not reproducible
)

11 · The confusion matrix and four metrics mathinteractive


Everything you report about a binary classifier comes out of four counts. Fix which label is "positive" first and write it down — for a complaint detector, positive = complaint. Swapping that convention swaps precision and recall, and reversed metrics in a report are a common and embarrassing error.



  
predicted 1predicted 0

  
actual 1TP
true positive		FN
false negative — a miss

  
actual 0FP
false positive — a false alarm		TN
true negative




Row = truth, column = prediction. Confirm the orientation of any matrix before you read it; scikit-learn's confusion_matrix uses this orientation, plotting libraries vary, and a transposed matrix silently exchanges FP with FN.




accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN)

precision = TP / (TP + FP)

recall = TP / (TP + FN)

F1 = 2 · precision · recall / (precision + recall)




Say each one in words, because the formulas are easy to permute and the sentences are not:


    
  
  • Accuracy — of everything I predicted, what fraction was right? The denominator is every example.
    • 
  
  • Precision — of the items I flagged, what fraction really was positive? The denominator is my positive predictions. It is the metric that governs how much of a reviewer's time you waste.
    • 
  
  • Recall — of the items that really were positive, what fraction did I catch? The denominator is the truth. It is the metric that governs how much you miss.
    • 
  
  • F1 — the harmonic mean of precision and recall. Harmonic, not arithmetic, so it is dragged toward the smaller of the two: precision 1.00 with recall 0.02 gives F1 = 0.039, whereas the arithmetic mean would have flattered it with 0.51.
    • 



Two degenerate cases to keep in mind. If the model predicts zero positives, precision is 0/0 — undefined. Libraries return 0 with a warning; a report that quietly prints 0.0 hides the fact that the model never fired. And F1 ignores TN entirely: it says nothing about the negative class, which is deliberate when negatives are the boring majority and misleading when they are not.

12 · The threshold is a decision, not a constant interactive


Your model outputs a score in [0, 1]. To emit a label you compare it to a threshold, and the default of 0.5 is a convention, not a result. Precision, recall, F1 and accuracy are all functions of that threshold; a claim about "the model's recall" without a stated threshold is incomplete.


The trade-off is mechanical and has no exceptions:


    
  
  • Raise the threshold. Fewer positive predictions. Every remaining one is backed by a higher score, so precision rises (weakly) and recall falls (weakly). At threshold 1.0 you predict nothing positive: recall 0, precision undefined.
    • 
  
  • Lower the threshold. More positive predictions. Recall rises, precision falls. At threshold 0.0 you flag everything: recall 1.0, precision equal to the positive-class prevalence.
    • 



Which end you want is a property of the application, not of the model:



  
ApplicationCost of a false positiveCost of a false negativeChoose

  
Auto-close obvious ticketsA customer's real issue is closed unreadA human handles a ticket that could have been automatedHigh threshold, high precision

  
Flag possible safety concerns for reviewA reviewer spends a minute on a benign messageA real concern is never seenLow threshold, high recall

  
Route to one of two queuesSymmetric — a mis-route either waySymmetric0.5, or whatever maximizes F1




Pick the threshold on a validation split and then report the test metrics at that fixed value. Sweeping the threshold on the test set and reporting the best point is a form of test-set fitting; the number you publish will not survive contact with new data.

13 · Why accuracy lies under class imbalance mathinteractive


Suppose 3% of incoming support messages are complaints and you build a complaint detector. Here is a classifier written in one line:


def predict(text):
    return 0    # never a complaint


It is 97% accurate. It has never been trained, it does not read its input, and it beats plenty of real models on the headline metric. Its recall is 0, its F1 is 0, and it is worth nothing.


This is not a curiosity. It is the single most common way a text-classification report misleads its reader, and the mechanism is arithmetic: accuracy is dominated by the majority class, so when the majority class is 97% of the data, accuracy is mostly a measurement of how often you predict the majority class.



The crossover, exactly


Let p be the positive-class prevalence, and let your real detector have true-positive rate TPR (that is, recall) and false-positive rate FPR. Then:



accuracy(majority) = 1 − p

accuracy(detector) = p · TPR + (1 − p)(1 − FPR)



The detector beats the do-nothing baseline on accuracy only when


p · TPR > (1 − p) · FPR   ⇔   p > FPR / (TPR + FPR)


Put in numbers. A detector with TPR = 0.70 and FPR = 0.05 needs prevalence above 0.05 / 0.75 = 6.7% before it is even accurate enough to justify itself against a constant. Below that, a genuinely useful detector — catching 70% of complaints while raising a false alarm on 5% of ordinary messages — scores worse on accuracy than a function that returns zero. Accuracy is not measuring what you care about.



What to report instead


    
  
  • Precision, recall and F1 on the positive class, with the threshold stated. These are exactly the quantities that ignore the mass of true negatives.
    • 
  
  • The confusion matrix itself. Four integers, no interpretation required, impossible to inflate.
    • 
  
  • Class prevalence in every split. If train, validation and test have different balances, your metrics are not comparable across them.
    • 
  
  • The majority-class baseline number. State it explicitly. It is the floor any real model has to clear, and printing it in your report proves you know where the floor is.
    • 



Handling imbalance during training is a separate question from measuring it: class weights in the loss, resampling the majority class down or the minority up, and threshold tuning after training all help. Threshold tuning is usually the cheapest and it never distorts the training distribution.

14 · Calibration: a softmax score is not a probability mathinteractive


Your service returns {"label": "complaint", "score": 0.94}. A reasonable consumer of that response reads it as "94 times out of 100, this is a complaint". That reading is a claim about the world, and it is usually false.


Softmax outputs are guaranteed to be non-negative and to sum to one. That makes them a valid probability distribution over the two classes. It does not make them correct probabilities. Nothing in the cross-entropy objective forces the number 0.94 to correspond to being right 94% of the time, and modern fine-tuned networks are reliably overconfident: among the predictions they score at 0.95, the fraction that are actually correct is typically lower.



The definition


A model is calibrated if, over all predictions it makes with confidence c, the fraction that are correct equals c:


P( y = ŷ | confidence = c ) = c   for all c


You measure it by binning. Sort the held-out predictions into 10 bins by confidence, and in each bin compare the mean confidence against the observed accuracy. Plot accuracy against confidence: a calibrated model lies on the diagonal, an overconfident model sits below it. Summarize with expected calibration error, the weighted mean gap:


ECE = Σb (nb / n) · | accuracy(b) − confidence(b) |


An ECE of 0.12 means that on average the model's stated confidence is off by 12 percentage points. The companion number is the Brier score, the mean squared difference between confidence and outcome — lower is better, and unlike ECE it also penalizes a model that is calibrated but uninformative.



The fix: temperature scaling


Divide the logits by a single learned scalar T before the softmax:


pi = softmax(zi / T)


Fit T on a held-out split — never on training data, and never on the split you report test metrics from — by minimizing negative log-likelihood over that one parameter. Three properties make this the standard first move:


    
  
  • T > 1 softens the distribution toward uniform; T < 1 sharpens it.
    • 
  
  • Dividing every logit by the same scalar cannot change which logit is largest, so accuracy, precision, recall and F1 at a fixed rank ordering are unchanged. You get honest probabilities for free.
    • 
  
  • One parameter cannot overfit a validation split of any reasonable size.
    • 



Platt scaling (fit a logistic regression on the logit) and isotonic regression (fit a monotone step function) are the other two standard choices; isotonic is more flexible and needs more validation data. Whichever you use, calibrate after training and report the ECE before and after, since that is the evidence that the probability in your API response means something.




  
Demo · Reliability diagram and temperature scaling

  
3,000 held-out predictions whose correctness is fixed. "Overconfidence" sharpens the model's stated confidence without changing a single prediction — exactly what over-training on a small corpus does. Then use temperature to pull it back onto the diagonal. Setting T equal to the overconfidence value restores calibration exactly, because both act linearly on the logit.

  

    

      

    

      

    
  

  

    
    

  






  Where this bites in production. Any downstream rule of the form "auto-resolve if score > 0.9" is a bet that the score means what it says. If the model is overconfident by 12 points, that rule is auto-resolving a category of message that is wrong roughly one time in five rather than one in ten. The rule looked safe on paper because nobody checked the calibration.





15 · Comparing the two models honestly


The comparison is the deliverable, not the transformer. A comparison is only worth reading if it is fair, and fairness is a checklist:


    
  
  1. Identical splits. Same rows in train, validation and test for both models, produced by one seeded split and saved. Not "the same 80/20" — the same rows.
    2. 
  
  2. Identical test-time labels and threshold policy. If you tuned the transformer's threshold on validation, tune the baseline's the same way. Comparing a tuned model against an untuned one is not a comparison.
    3. 
  
  3. No leakage in either direction. Fit the vectorizer on train only. Deduplicate near-identical documents before splitting, or the same text lands on both sides and both models look better than they are.
    4. 
  
  4. Report the cost as well as the score. Parameters, training time, per-request latency, memory. A 2-point F1 gain that costs 200× the inference budget is a finding, not a failure — state it and let the reader decide.
    5. 
  
  5. Report the same metrics for both, at the same threshold, with the confusion matrices printed. Four integers per model make selective reporting impossible.
    6. 
  
  6. Fix and record the seeds. Fine-tuning on a small corpus has real run-to-run variance; a single run of each model is weak evidence, and if you can afford three seeds, report the spread.
    7. 




Error analysis is where the actual learning happens


Metrics tell you how much you are wrong. Only reading the errors tells you why. Pull the examples where the two models disagree and sort them into groups. The categories that usually appear:



  
GroupTypical causeWhat to do

  
Transformer right, baseline wrong — negation or word orderThe exact blindness of section 6Quote one as evidence for the transformer in your report

  
Transformer right, baseline wrong — unseen vocabularyClosed vocabulary versus subwordsSame — this is the transfer-learning payoff

  
Baseline right, transformer wrong — a rare, decisive keywordTruncation, or the encoder smoothing over a term the linear model weighted heavilyCheck max_length; consider an ensemble

  
Both wrong, and the label looks defensibleLabel noise or an ambiguous annotation guidelineFix the guideline; this caps both models' ceiling

  
Both right for the wrong reasonA shortcut in the data — a template phrase that co-occurs with the labelTest on out-of-distribution examples; report the risk




That last row deserves a paragraph. Shortcut learning is the phenomenon where a model achieves high held-out scores by exploiting a superficial correlation that holds in your corpus and nowhere else — a boilerplate footer that appears only in one class, an artifact of how the data was collected, a length difference between classes. Held-out accuracy will not catch it, because the shortcut is present in the held-out split too. The only reliable detectors are reading errors by hand, testing on data collected differently, and checking whether the model still works when you delete the suspicious cue.




  Minimum honest report for the topic project. For each of the two models: the confusion matrix, accuracy, precision, recall and F1 on the same held-out split at a stated threshold; the majority-class accuracy for reference; the ECE before and after calibration; training and inference cost; the seed; and the vectorizer or tokenizer configuration in full. Then three concrete input types where the transformer wins, one worked example each. Anything less is an assertion rather than a result.
