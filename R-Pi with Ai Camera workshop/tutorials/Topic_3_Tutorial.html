Topic 3 · Image Restoration and Reconstruction

Topic 3 asks you to restore a degraded image — but the AI Camera only produces clean, already-in-focus digital captures. This tutorial covers the synthetic degradation workaround the assignment already permits: you degrade your own photo on purpose, then restore it, so you have ground truth to measure against.

Scope: noise models, inverse filtering, Wiener filtering, inpainting, and the PSNR/SSIM quality metrics used to evaluate the result.

1. Why you degrade your own image on purpose

A found "degraded" photo has no known ground truth — you cannot measure how well you restored it because you never had the clean original. The fix, and the approach this assignment explicitly allows: capture a clean photo with the AI Camera, then apply a known degradation (noise, blur, or a masked-out region) in code. Now you hold both the clean original and the degraded version, so PSNR/SSIM comparisons are meaningful.

Make this explicit in your notebook: state clearly which image is the ground truth and which is the synthetically degraded copy — it is easy for a reader (and a grader) to lose track of which is which.

3. Inverse filtering vs. Wiener filtering

Inverse filtering tries to undo a known blur by dividing in the frequency domain — mathematically clean, but it amplifies noise catastrophically at frequencies where the blur kernel is near zero. Wiener filtering adds a noise-power term to that division, trading a small amount of sharpness for dramatically better noise stability. In practice, Wiener is almost always the one you want unless the image is genuinely noise-free.

4. Inpainting: filling in a missing region

Inpainting reconstructs a masked-out region using the surrounding pixels — OpenCV's cv2.inpaint() implements two algorithms (Telea and Navier-Stokes) that propagate nearby texture and color inward. This is a fundamentally different problem from denoising: there is no "true" answer, only a plausible one. 

mask = np.zeros(frame_gray.shape, np.uint8)
cv2.rectangle(mask, (100,100), (180,160), 255, -1)  # region to remove/reconstruct
restored = cv2.inpaint(frame_gray, mask, 3, cv2.INPAINT_TELEA)

5. PSNR and SSIM: putting a number on "better"

PSNR (Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio) measures raw pixel-level difference in decibels — higher is better, but it doesn't always match human perception. SSIM (Structural Similarity Index) compares local luminance, contrast, and structure, and correlates much better with what a person would call "looks similar." Report both: a restoration can score well on one and poorly on the other.
