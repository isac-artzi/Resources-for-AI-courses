Topic 6 · Motion Estimation and Tracking

Topic 6 is where the AI Camera's native video streaming becomes the whole point — capture a video stream, detect trackable corner features with Shi-Tomasi, and track them frame to frame with Lucas-Kanade optical flow.

Scope: video capture, the Shi-Tomasi corner detector, Lucas-Kanade optical flow, drawing motion vectors, and evaluating tracking performance.

1. Capturing a video stream on the Pi 5

Unlike Topics 1–5, which mostly work from a single still, this topic needs a sequence of frames. The Cam 6 (Clip Record & Playback) project template's record-then-read-back pattern is exactly what you reuse here: record to a .264 stream, convert with ffmpeg if needed, then read it back frame by frame. 

picam2.start_and_record_video("clip.h264", duration=6)
# then, frame by frame:
cap = cv2.VideoCapture("clip.mp4")
ok, frame = cap.read()
gray_prev = cv2.cvtColor(frame, cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)

2. Shi-Tomasi: choosing which points to track

Optical flow needs distinctive points to lock onto — flat, textureless regions can't be tracked reliably because any nearby point looks the same. The Shi-Tomasi corner detector finds points where intensity changes sharply in two directions at once (true corners), which are far more trackable than edges or flat regions. 

corners = cv2.goodFeaturesToTrack(gray_prev, maxCorners=100,
                                   qualityLevel=0.3, minDistance=7)

3. Lucas-Kanade: following those points frame to frame

Lucas-Kanade assumes small motion and near-constant brightness in a local window around each tracked point, then solves for the displacement that best explains the change between consecutive frames. It fails when motion is large (the point "jumps" out of the search window) or lighting changes abruptly — both are worth calling out as limitations in your analysis. 

new_pts, status, err = cv2.calcOpticalFlowPyrLK(
    gray_prev, gray_next, corners, None)
tracked = new_pts[status.flatten() == 1]

4. Drawing and reading motion vectors

Draw a line from each point's previous position to its new position, with a small circle at the current position — this is the standard "motion vector" visualization. Longer lines mean faster apparent motion; a cluster of vectors all pointing the same direction usually indicates camera motion rather than object motion.

5. Evaluating tracking quality

Report three things: accuracy (do tracked points visually stay on their real-world feature?), computational complexity (frames per second achieved), and noise resilience (do points jitter on a static scene, or drift over a long clip?). Sensor/context fusion — e.g. combining tracking with a motion or proximity sensor — is worth discussing even if you don't have the hardware to implement it directly.
