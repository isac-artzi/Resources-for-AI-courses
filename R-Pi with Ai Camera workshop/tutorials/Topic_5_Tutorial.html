Topic 5 · Image Registration and Alignment

Topic 5 aligns two images of the same scene taken under slightly different conditions. On the AI Camera this means two captures with the camera physically moved slightly between them — which only produces a meaningful, debuggable result if focus and framing are controlled deliberately.

Scope: manual tie-point selection, affine/bilinear transformation models, applying the transform, and comparing interpolation methods.

1. Two prerequisites this topic silently depends on

Lock focus before you shoot either image. The IMX500 has no software autofocus — focus is set purely mechanically, with the physical focus tool, turning the nubs toward the notches, across a 20 cm–∞ range. If you refocus (even slightly) between your two captures, the geometric relationship between them stops being a clean transform and tie-point matching gets noticeably harder to reason about.
Put a grid or checkerboard pattern on your capture surface. A blank whiteboard gives you almost no distinguishable tie points. Draw or tape a grid onto the whiteboard prop before shooting — this is a documented failure mode, and it fails silently: you'll get four "tie points" that all look plausible and produce a garbage transform.

2. Selecting and matching tie points

A tie point is a pixel coordinate that identifies the same physical feature in both images — a grid intersection, a corner of the whiteboard, a marked dot. You need at least four, well spread across the frame (not clustered in one corner), because an affine transform has six free parameters and four point-pairs (eight coordinate values) is the practical minimum to solve for them reliably. 

ref_pts  = np.float32([[120,80], [500,90], [130,420], [510,430]])
dist_pts = np.float32([[140,95], [515,100], [150,430], [520,440]])

3. Affine vs. bilinear transformation models

An affine transform (translation + rotation + scale + shear) is the right model when the camera moved but didn't change perspective much — a small lateral or rotational shift, which is exactly what "move the camera slightly" produces. A bilinear (or higher-order polynomial) model can absorb more complex, non-uniform distortion, but needs more tie points and is easier to overfit with only four. 

M = cv2.getAffineTransform(ref_pts[:3], dist_pts[:3])   # 3 points -> affine
aligned = cv2.warpAffine(distorted_img, M, (w, h))

4. Interpolation: what fills the gaps

Warping an image maps output pixel locations back to non-integer source coordinates — interpolation decides how to sample there. Nearest-neighbor is fast but blocky. Bilinear smooths using the four nearest source pixels. Bicubic uses a wider 4×4 neighborhood for smoother results at higher cost. Compare at least two on the same warp and describe the visible difference.

5. Evaluating registration accuracy

Overlay the aligned image on the reference with 50% transparency, or compute a per-pixel difference image — sharp double edges or a bright difference image mean the alignment is off; a clean single edge and a near-black difference image mean it worked.
